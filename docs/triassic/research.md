## Corrections that changed the design

- **Helicoprion has no Triassic record.** Its peer-reviewed range is Artinskian–Roadian
  (Early–Middle Permian). It is on the roster as a labelled relict at the user's request; the
  real Triassic eugeneodonts are *Fadenia* and *Caseodus* (Olenekian, British Columbia).
- **Adult Shonisaurus had teeth** and ate vertebrates (Kelley et al. 2022); the toothless
  squid-slurper is superseded. The suction-feeding stories for the toothless shastasaurids and
  the baleen story for Hupehsuchus both have published rebuttals and are presented as debated.
- **Tanystropheus' neck was stiff**: a rigid beam swung from its base, striking with a sideways
  head-snap, not a swan's lunge; and two specimens preserve necks bitten clean through
  (Spiekman & Mujal 2023). Both shape the shore-animal design. It is an archosauromorph, not a
  dinosaur.
- *Hybodus hauffianus* is Jurassic; the Triassic hybodonts are *Hybodus* spp., *Acrodus* and
  *Palaeobates*. *Cymbospondylus petrinus* is from Nevada, not Monte San Giorgio.
- The "flat clams on log rafts" idea is now the minority view; Daonella and Halobia were
  dysoxic-floor epibenthos. *Traumatocrinus* log rafts are solidly supported.

### 1. *Nothosaurus giganteus* and *N. mirabilis*

**Species / age / locality.** *N. mirabilis* Münster 1834 (type species), Upper Muschelkalk, late Anisian, Bayreuth region, Bavaria; *N. giganteus* Münster 1834, Upper Muschelkalk of Germany (rare at Bayreuth) and the latest Anisian Besano Formation / Grenzbitumenzone at Monte San Giorgio (formerly *Paranothosaurus amsleri*). The genus ranges across the Tethys from Europe and North Africa to China.

**Adult length.** *N. mirabilis*: the holotype, not fully grown, reconstructs at 290–320 cm (Klein et al. 2022); ~3–4 m is the usual quoted adult. *N. giganteus* (with *N. zhangi*) is the largest, 5–7 m.

**Distinctive anatomy.**
- Long, flat, low skull with elongated upper temporal fenestrae; slender rostrum with five interlocking premaxillary fangs per side plus small maxillary teeth — a fish-trap dentition.
- Long neck (19 cervicals in *N. mirabilis*), long trunk with high neural spines and zygosphene–zygantrum articulations that stiffen and power lateral undulation.
- Humerus with an extremely broad, flat, wing-shaped shaft and a flattened ulna; large nothosaur humeri have a remarkably thin cortex, "comparable to aerial birds", read as an adaptation to torsional loading in the flipper.
- Webbed, paddle-like feet with retained digits; long tail probably finned.
- Three humerus morphotypes in Lower Muschelkalk material (Winterswijk) may record strong sexual dimorphism or hidden taxa (Klein et al. 2015).

**Behaviour.**
- Feeding: piscivore/small-prey snatcher — **supported** (dentition). Yunnan trackways (2014) show paired paddle scrapes interpreted as rowing along the seabed to flush benthic prey — **inferred**.
- Locomotion: trunk/tail undulation for fast swimming, forelimb paddling or "flying-rowing" for slow cruising and acceleration (Klein et al. 2022; Krahl 2021) — **inferred**. Seal-like amphibious haul-out — **speculative** (traditional, untested).
- Reproduction: viviparity, from embryo finds in nothosaurids at Monte San Giorgio and life-history modelling (Griebeler 2019) — **inferred**.

**Fun to play.** Two gears: a slow rowing "search" mode over the sand versus a tail-driven burst; a fang-lined snap that pins slippery fish. *N. giganteus* is the top Muschelkalk predator — a 6 m apex form to grow into.

Sources: [Klein et al. 2022, *N. mirabilis* redescription (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9422981/) · [Klein et al. 2015, *N. marchicus* swimming/humerus morphotypes (PalZ)](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s12542-015-0273-5) · [Krahl 2021, paraxial swimming in nothosauroids (PalZ)](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s12542-021-00563-w) · [Griebeler 2019, live-bearing in *Nothosaurus*](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/pala.12425) · [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nothosaurus)

### 2. *Keichousaurus hui*

**Age / locality.** Ladinian (Middle Triassic), Zhuganpo Member, Falang Formation, Xingyi area, Guizhou (also Yunnan), South China — the Xingyi Fauna.

**Adult length.** 10–30 cm total length; maximum ~30 cm, females to ~23 cm. Snout–vent: males average 16.1 cm, females up to 14.9 cm; maturity at ~12.2–12.6 cm SVL (Xue et al. 2015).

**Distinctive anatomy.**
- Tiny head, long serpentine neck, long tail; lizard-like proportions.
- Broad, flattened ulna and strongly built humerus — the forelimb is the propulsive limb; limbs paddle-shaped, laterally set, no sternum.
- Recurved, needle-like teeth for grasping fish.
- Pachyostotic (thickened) ribs and dorsal vertebrae as ballast.
- Sexual dimorphism: males have a relatively longer, more robust humerus and femur and larger body; skeletal ratios separate the sexes (Cheng et al. 2004; Xue et al. 2015).

**Behaviour.**
- Reproduction: viviparity — **supported**. Two gravid females with articulated embryos in the body cavity (Cheng, Wu & Ji 2004, *Nature*); a mobile pelvis is argued to have eased live birth; a regurgitalite of juveniles is also known.
- Feeding: piscivore — **supported** (gut contents, Li et al. 2023).
- Locomotion: forelimb-dominant paddling with tail assist in shallow, near-shore water — **inferred**.
- Social/shoaling: it is the most abundant reptile in the Xingyi biota (>1,600 specimens studied), often complete and articulated, so dense populations are **supported**; actual schooling behaviour is **speculative**.
- Sexual selection: male-biased size dimorphism and adult sex ratios point to competition among males (Liu et al. 2015) — **inferred**.

**Fun to play.** The classic hatchling body: small, fast, numerous, born live into a crowd of kin. A "swarm of Keichousaurus" is the natural shallow-water prey field, and a player one could grow through male/female builds (sturdier male limbs vs. a gravid female that must reach safety).

Sources: [Cheng, Wu & Ji 2004, *Nature*](https://www.nature.com/articles/nature03050) · [Xue et al. 2015, sexual dimorphism & allometry (APP)](https://www.app.pan.pl/article/item/app000062013.html) · [Liu et al. 2015, adult sex ratio (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4614760/) · [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Keichousaurus)

### 3. *Neusticosaurus* (brief)

**Age / locality.** Anisian–Ladinian boundary to early Ladinian, Monte San Giorgio (Switzerland/Italy): three species (*N. pusillus*, *N. peyeri*, *N. edwardsii*) stacked in successive horizons of the Lower Meride Limestone, hundreds of complete skeletons (Sander 1989).

**Length.** ~20–40 cm for *N. pusillus*/*N. peyeri*; *N. edwardsii* is the largest, to roughly 1.2 m.

**Distinctive anatomy.** Small skull, moderate neck, pachyostotic ribs; sexes differ in humerus shape/size; the lineage shows progressive size increase and a documented evolutionary series between horizons.

**Behaviour.** Viviparity — **supported**: an isolated 51 mm embryo with no eggshell, only 22 % of adult length (Sander 1988, *Science*), plus further embryos, three clustered together, from the same basin. Growth: lamellar-zonal bone with annual cycles; maturity in a few years (Hugi et al.; FR 2018 modelling) — **supported**. Shallow-water fish/invertebrate feeder using forelimbs and tail — **inferred**.

**Fun to play.** The Alpine sibling of *Keichousaurus*: a Monte San Giorgio "nursery" species and a size ladder within one genus.

Sources: [Sander 1988, embryo (*Science*)](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.3340859) · [Sander 1989, Monte San Giorgio pachypleurosaurids (PubMed)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/2575768/) · [Growth/dimorphism modelling, Fossil Record 2018](https://fr.copernicus.org/articles/21/137/2018/)

### 4. *Rhaeticosaurus mertensi*

**Age / locality.** Rhaetian, Late Triassic, ~205 Ma; Exter Formation, ~3.5 m below the Triassic–Jurassic boundary, clay pit at Bonenburg, Westphalia, Germany. Found by Michael Mertens in 2013; described by Wintrich et al. 2017 (*Science Advances*). The oldest and only Triassic plesiosaur skeleton.

**Adult length.** Reconstructed 237 cm (part of the neck lost to quarrying); a juvenile/subadult, so adults were somewhat larger.

**Distinctive anatomy.**
- Fully plesiosaurian body plan already in place: barrel trunk, four hydrofoil flippers with hyperphalangy, plate-like pectoral and pelvic girdles, short tail.
- Recovered as a basal pliosaurid — a comparatively short-necked, large-headed lineage.
- Bone histology: fibrolamellar bone with few growth marks, i.e. fast, continuous growth to near-adult size.
- Head disintegrated; girdles and limbs preserved.
- Dense gastral basket.

**Behaviour.**
- Locomotion: four-flipper underwater flight, tail only for steering — **inferred** (the body plan; Wintrich et al. compare penguins and sea turtles).
- Physiology: rapid growth read as evidence of elevated metabolic rate / endothermy, letting plesiosaurs colonise cooler open water and survive the end-Triassic extinction — **inferred**.
- Feeding: fish and cephalopods in open water — **inferred** from pliosaurid affinity (no skull).
- Reproduction: live birth, by analogy with the Cretaceous *Polycotylus* gravid specimen (O'Keefe & Chiappe 2011) — **inferred**.

**Fun to play.** The first "flying" body: all four limbs beat together, no tail thrust, a totally different feel from the undulating nothosaurs. Being warm-blooded lets it cross cold, deep biomes others avoid.

Sources: [Wintrich et al. 2017 summary, Univ. Bonn](https://www.uni-bonn.de/en/university/press-and-communications/press-service/archive-press-releases/2017/302-2017) · [Novataxa summary of the paper](http://novataxa.blogspot.com/2017/12/rhaeticosaurus.html) · [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rhaeticosaurus)

### 5. *Pistosaurus longaevus*

**Age / locality.** Anisian–Ladinian, Upper Muschelkalk of the Germanic Basin (Bayreuth, Germany) and France.

**Adult length.** ~3 m.

**Distinctive anatomy.**
- Skull nothosaur-like in the palate but with plesiosaur-like braincase and a long, slender snout; splint-like nasals excluded from the naris.
- Long neck; stiff vertebral column (plesiosaur trait) rather than the flexible nothosaur trunk.
- Massive scapula and expanded coracoid; femur longer than humerus; pelvis still primitive.
- Limbs already paddle-like flippers with reduced hand mobility.
- Humerus histology: osteosclerotic (medulla filled with calcified cartilage) yet with fibrolamellar, fast-growing bone unlike *Nothosaurus*' lamellar-zonal bone (Krahl et al. 2013).

**Behaviour.**
- Locomotion: the origin of sub-aquatic flight — bilateral, simultaneous strokes, hindlimb-dominated at this stage (Krahl et al. 2013) — **inferred**.
- Growth/metabolism: fast growth suggests a higher metabolic rate than nothosaurs — **inferred**.
- Feeding: open-water fish eater with slender jaws — **inferred**.
- Reproduction: viviparity by phylogenetic bracketing — **speculative**.

**Fun to play.** The transitional body: a nothosaur head on a plesiosaur engine. Good "mid-tier" mover — faster and more sustained in open water than a nothosaur, less able to turn or work the bottom.

Sources: [Krahl, Klein & Sander 2013, BMC Evol. Biol.](https://bmcecolevol.biomedcentral.com/articles/10.1186/1471-2148-13-123) · [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pistosaurus)

### 6. *Placodus gigas*

**Age / locality.** Anisian–Ladinian (~245–235 Ma), Lower and Upper Muschelkalk: Winterswijk (Netherlands), Hünfeld, Bindlach/Bayreuth, Steinsfurt (Germany, the only articulated skeleton, 1913), also France, Poland; a related species in China.

**Adult length.** 2.5–3 m (Wikipedia; Neenan gives 1–2 m for the group's unarmoured placodontoids), stocky trunk, long tail.

**Distinctive anatomy.**
- Procumbent, chisel-like premaxillary incisors that jut forward from the snout.
- Broad, flat, bean-shaped crushing teeth on the maxillae and huge plates on the palatines; heavy, akinetic skull.
- Dense, pachyostotic bones and a tight basket of angled gastralia armouring the belly and acting as ballast.
- A single row of bony knobs (osteoderms) along the spine; otherwise unarmoured.
- Short, relatively unmodified limbs with (probably) webbed feet; laterally flattened tail.
- Inner-ear canals compressed and elongate, a signal of habitual swimming; snout held ~20° down when feeding (Neenan).

**Behaviour.**
- Feeding: durophagy on bivalves, brachiopods and crustaceans, prised from the substrate with the incisors and crushed on the palate — **supported** (dentition, wear). Diedrich's "sea cow" macroalgae-grazing hypothesis is a minority view — **speculative**.
- Locomotion: negatively buoyant bottom-walker/slow tail-sculler in shallow shelf seas — **inferred**.
- Amphibious hauling out — **speculative**; poorly suited to land.
- Tooth replacement: unique posterior-to-anterior replacement in placodonts (Neenan et al. 2014) — **supported**.

**Fun to play.** A tank: slow, heavy, immune to most bites, that "mines" shell beds with a crunch. Ballast lets it walk the seabed; the trade-off is no burst speed.

Sources: [Neenan, Fossil Focus: Placodonts](https://www.palaeontologyonline.com/?p=3247) · [Neenan et al. 2014, placodont tooth replacement (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3981503/) · [Klein et al. 2022, *Placodus* dentaries (PMC)](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9132834/) · [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Placodus)

### 7. *Henodus chelyops*

**Age / locality.** Early Carnian, Late Triassic; Oberer Gipskeuper at Tübingen-Lustnau, Baden-Württemberg — an ephemeral lacustrine to restricted shallow-marine, brackish (at times hypersaline) lagoon. The only placodont from non-marine deposits; a possible cranium is now reported from the Upper Triassic of Portugal (Ruciński et al. 2025).

**Adult length.** ~1 m; skull ~16 cm.

**Distinctive anatomy.**
- Extremely broad, square, flat body with a carapace wider than long, a plastron below, and a mosaic of many small polygonal osteoderms (far more elements than a turtle shell), fused to the spine.
- Nearly toothless: one pair of small, oval, concave crushing teeth on the palatines and one pair on the dentaries; premaxillary/maxillary teeth lost, jaw edges horny/beak-like.
- Rostrum with a sharp denticulate cutting edge and paired grooves with striations read as attachment sites for baleen-like keratin fringes.
- Very short, wide, flat skull; eyes far forward; deep robust lower jaw suited to rapid depression.
- Small, weak limbs.

**Behaviour.**
- Feeding: debated — filter/suction feeder (Rieppel 2002; baleen-like structures), aquatic herbivore scraping algae/plants (Chun et al. 2016 *Science Advances* comparison with *Atopodentatus*), or a mixed diet crushing 3 mm crustaceans and gastropods found in its matrix (Pommery et al. 2021). Small-particle diet — **supported**; specific mechanism — **inferred**.
- Habitat: euryhaline lagoon specialist — **supported** (sedimentology).
- Locomotion: sluggish benthic paddler, shell as ballast — **inferred**.

**Fun to play.** A niche oddity for a brackish shore biome: an armoured, square "hoover" that grazes plant/plankton mats no one else can use. Nearly unkillable but barely able to swim — pure defensive gameplay.

Sources: [Pommery et al. 2021, *Henodus* dentition (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8256584/) · [Chun et al. 2016, earliest herbivorous marine reptile (*Sci. Adv.*)](https://www.science.org/doi/full/10.1126/sciadv.1501659) · [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henodus)

### 8. *Psephoderma alpinum* and *Cyamodus*

**Age / locality.** *Psephoderma alpinum* Meyer 1858: Norian–Rhaetian (~210 Ma), Calcare di Zorzino (Lombardy, Italy), Kössen beds of the Alps (Schesaplana, Graubünden); *P. anglicum* in the Rhaetian of England. *Cyamodus* (six species incl. *C. rostratus*, *C. munsteri*, *C. hildegardis*, *C. kuhnschnyderi*): Anisian–Ladinian, Muschelkalk of Bavaria/Germanic Basin, Monte San Giorgio, northern Italy, and China (*C. orientalis*).

**Adult length.** *Psephoderma*: up to 180 cm (largest Zorzino specimen), of which the tail is ~80 cm. *Cyamodus*: 1.3–1.5 m and 20–25 kg; *C. rostratus* ~0.9 m.

**Distinctive anatomy.**
- Two-part armour: a large dorsal shield over the trunk and a separate smaller pelvic shield, with a gap over the sacrum that keeps the hips mobile; scutes hexagonal/rounded, partly fused, in *Psephoderma* streamlined and keeled.
- *Psephoderma*: very narrow, elongate, toothless rostrum; *Cyamodus*: broad heart-shaped skull with a short snout.
- Few, huge crushing teeth: *Cyamodus* one premaxillary, two maxillary, giant pterygoid/palatine plates; *Psephoderma* rounded posterior crushers only.
- Paddles with separate digits, not flippers; long stiff tail (with a caudal plate in *Psephoderma*) for balance.
- Unilateral tooth replacement, one side of the mouth at a time (Neenan et al. 2014).

**Behaviour.**
- Feeding: durophagy — **supported**. *Psephoderma* probed soft sediment with its rostrum like a ray, then crushed — **inferred** (Renesto & Tintori 1995). *Cyamodus* uprooted shellfish with the jaws — **inferred**.
- Locomotion: carapace read as hydrodynamic ballast rather than pure defence; swam at least as well as soft-shelled turtles — **inferred**.
- Habitat: shallow carbonate platforms and intraplatform basins; rare on land — **inferred**.

**Fun to play.** Two flavours of shelled bottom-feeder: the beak-probing sand-sifter (*Psephoderma*) versus the broad-jawed shell-cruncher (*Cyamodus*). Split armour makes for a clear "soft spot" mechanic at the hips and neck.

Sources: [Renesto & Tintori 1995, *Psephoderma* functional morphology (RIPS)](https://riviste.unimi.it/index.php/RIPS/article/view/8564) · [Neenan & Scheyer 2014, new *Psephoderma* from Schesaplana (Swiss J. Geosci.)](https://sjg.springeropen.com/articles/10.1007/s00015-014-0173-9) · [Wikipedia: *Cyamodus*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cyamodus) · [Wikipedia: *Psephoderma*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Psephoderma)

### 9. *Placochelys placodonta* (brief)

**Age / locality.** Carnian, Late Triassic; holotype skull from the "Lower Keuper" (Veszprém Marl) of Jeruzsálem Hill, Veszprém, Hungary (Jaekel 1902); also Germany.

**Adult length.** ~90 cm.

**Distinctive anatomy.** Turtle-like flat carapace of knobbly plates; compact triangular skull with a narrow toothless beak; a few broad crushing plates on the palate; paddle-shaped limbs with free digits; short tail. Closest relative of *Psephoderma* (Placochelyidae).

**Behaviour.** Shellfish crusher on carbonate ramps — **supported** (dentition); nearshore paddler and possible haul-out to bask, like a sea turtle — **speculative**.

**Fun to play.** A small, early "sea turtle" analogue — the compact, cheap-to-play shelled body before *Psephoderma*'s larger, longer-tailed version.

Sources: [Ősi et al. 2020, placodont remains from Hungary (Palaeobio. Palaeoenv.)](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s12549-020-00432-5) · [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Placochelys)

## Triassic ichthyosaurs and ichthyosauriforms — research notes

Web access worked; every subject below was checked against at least two online sources (primary paper where available, plus museum/press and Wikipedia as secondary). Confidence labels: **supported** = direct fossil evidence or published quantitative analysis; **inferred** = reasonable reading of anatomy with published backing; **speculative** = plausible but contested or untested.

One correction to the brief: *Cymbospondylus petrinus* is a Nevada species (Fossil Hill Member); only *C. buchseri* is from Monte San Giorgio.

### 1. Shonisaurus popularis

**Species / age / locality.** *Shonisaurus popularis* Camp 1976. Luning Formation, latest Carnian (~230–227 Ma), West Union Canyon, Berlin-Ichthyosaur State Park (BISP), Nevada; ~37 individuals excavated from 1954 by Camp and Welles; Nevada's state fossil since 1984.

**Adult length.** 13.5–15 m, ~22–30 t (Wikipedia, citing Kosch 1990 and later work). The old "S. sikanniensis" at 21 m is now *Shastasaurus*.

**Distinctive anatomy.**
- Body far slimmer than the 1970s "whale-blimp" reconstructions — Kosch (1990) re-measured ribs and girdle spacing — but still deeper-bodied than other shastasaurids.
- Long, narrow snout; teeth once thought confined to juveniles, but Kelley et al. (2022) report robust, cutting (sectorial) teeth in adults.
- Long, narrow flippers; long tail without the crescent fluke of Jurassic forms, no dorsal fin evidence.
- Very large size with an unusually deep ribcage for a shastasaurid.

**Behaviour.**
- Feeding: macrophagous apex predator — gut contents with vertebrate bone and cephalopod material (**supported**, Kelley et al. 2022); the earlier "toothless squid-slurper" view is superseded.
- Grouping: dozens of adults plus embryos/neonates but no juveniles in a low-diversity, moderately deep basin with nothing to eat → a philopatric birthing ground used across hundreds of thousands of years (**inferred**, Kelley et al. 2022). Earlier ideas: mass stranding, red-tide poisoning (Hogler), and McMenamin's "kraken" (rejected).
- Locomotion: long-bodied undulatory swimmer, probably slow cruiser (**inferred**).
- Reproduction: viviparous, embryos present (**supported**).

**Fun to play.** A pod-based giant: the play loop could be a seasonal migration to a nursery where you defend newborns, with grouping as a mechanic rather than a lone-apex fantasy. Toothed adult vs toothless-myth also gives a satisfying "it actually bites" reveal.

Sources: Kelley et al. 2022, *Current Biology* https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cub.2022.11.005 ; Smithsonian/UNR press https://www.unr.edu/nevada-today/news/2022/ichthyosaur-smithsonian-collaboration ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shonisaurus

### 2. Shastasaurus sikanniensis and Ichthyotitan severnensis

**Species / age / locality.** *Shastasaurus sikanniensis* (Nicholls & Manabe 2004): Pardonet Formation, middle Norian (~210 Ma), Sikanni Chief River, British Columbia. *Ichthyotitan severnensis* (Lomax et al. 2024): Westbury Mudstone Formation, latest Rhaetian (~202 Ma), Blue Anchor and Lilstock, Somerset, UK.

**Adult length.** *S. sikanniensis* ~21 m, ~81 t — the largest reasonably complete ichthyosaur. *Ichthyotitan* known only from two surangulars (holotype >2 m long); scaled against *S. sikanniensis* gives ~26 m, against *Besanosaurus* ~22 m; ~25 m is the authors' headline figure (**inferred**, wide error bars — a jawbone is a poor proxy).

**Distinctive anatomy.**
- Extremely slender body: ribcage <2 m deep despite >7 m between flipper girdles (*Shastasaurus*).
- Short, toothless snout in *Shastasaurus* (edentulous even as adults).
- Small flippers relative to body; long, low tail; no dorsal fin.
- *Ichthyotitan* surangular with a distinct dorsal ridge/groove; histology shows bone still growing at death.
- Aust Cliff "dinosaur" bones reinterpreted as giant ichthyosaur jaw fragments.

**Behaviour.**
- Feeding: Sander et al. (2011) proposed beaked-whale-style suction feeding on soft cephalopods (toothless, "short" snout, "enlarged" hyoid). Motani et al. (2013) tested this quantitatively and rejected it: hyoids short and slender, snouts not actually short, no ichthyosaur fits the suction-feeder profile (**contested; criticism currently favoured**). Alternative: ram/raptorial feeding on soft prey, possibly orca-like (**speculative**).
- Locomotion: undulatory, slow-cruising giant (**inferred**).
- Diving: unknown; *Shonisaurus*-like deep-basin habitat suggests open-water pelagic life (**speculative**).
- Reproduction: viviparous by phylogenetic bracket (**inferred**).

**Fun to play.** The "whale" of the Triassic — enormous, toothless, slow, vulnerable only to nothing; a size-tier apex whose challenge is feeding volume, not fighting. Also a nice endgame reveal: something even bigger (*Ichthyotitan*) exists only as a rumour of jawbones.

Sources: Lomax et al. 2024, *PLOS ONE* https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0300289 ; Motani et al. 2013, *PLOS ONE* https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC3859474/ ; Sander et al. 2011 https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0019480 ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shastasaurus ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ichthyotitan

### 3. Cymbospondylus youngorum

**Species / age / locality.** *Cymbospondylus youngorum* Sander et al. 2021. Fossil Hill Member, Favret Formation, Augusta Mountains, Nevada; Anisian, ~246 Ma. Holotype (LACM DI 157871): ~2 m skull, partial vertebral column, shoulder girdle, forefin — housed at the Natural History Museum of Los Angeles County.

**Adult length.** ~17.65 m; mass estimate up to ~44.7 t (Sander et al. 2021) — sperm-whale scale, the largest known animal of its time on land or sea, only ~8 Myr after the first ichthyosaurs.

**Distinctive anatomy.**
- Very long, narrow snout with small eyes for the skull size.
- Homodont, conical, ridged teeth along the full jaw — a toothed giant, unlike shastasaurids.
- Elongate trunk with a long, weakly differentiated tail; no dorsal fin.
- Humerus the second-largest recorded in any ichthyosaur.
- Basal, "primitive" body plan (eel-like proportions) scaled to giant size.

**Behaviour.**
- Feeding: macropredator — squid, fish and probably other marine reptiles; the Fossil Hill fauna's size spread rivals a modern marine-mammal community (**inferred**, Sander et al. 2021). Gigantism modelled as a food-web outcome without modern primary producers (ammonoids and conodonts at the base).
- Locomotion: anguilliform/undulatory cruising, not tuna-like thunniform (**inferred**).
- Diving/breathing: nothing direct; large size implies long breath-hold (**speculative**).
- Reproduction: viviparous (**inferred**, bracket).

**Fun to play.** The earliest "big-body" unlock in a rapid-gigantism story: an evolutionary race where the size ladder climbs absurdly fast. A long toothed jaw on an eel-like body makes it a lunge-and-grip predator rather than a suction feeder — a good contrast with *Shastasaurus*.

Sources: Sander et al. 2021, *Science* https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.abf5787 ; NHMLAC press https://nhm.org/press/earths-first-giant ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cymbospondylus

### 4. Cymbospondylus buchseri / C. petrinus (brief)

**Species / age / locality.** *C. petrinus* Leidy 1868 — the type species, Fossil Hill Member, Nevada, Anisian; ~9–12.5 m. *C. buchseri* Sander 1989 — Besano Formation (Anisian/Ladinian boundary), Cava Tre Fontane, Monte San Giorgio, Switzerland; single holotype PIMUZ T 4351 (anterior half, 3D-preserved).

**Adult length.** *C. buchseri* ~5–5.5 m, but histology shows the holotype was still growing; *C. petrinus* ~9.3–12.6 m.

**Distinctive anatomy.**
- *C. buchseri*: comparatively high, broad rostrum; long slender body.
- Conical ridged teeth, small orbits, no dorsal fin (genus-wide).
- Long tail with a low fin, no lunate fluke.
- Other Nevada species (*C. nichollsi* ~7.6 m, *C. duelferi* ~4.5 m) show the genus spanned a wide size range in one fauna.

**Behaviour.**
- Feeding: *C. buchseri* stomach contents are arm hooks and beaks of numerous phragmoteuthid coleoids — a squid-eater (**supported**).
- Locomotion: undulatory (**inferred**).
- Reproduction: viviparous (**inferred**).

**Fun to play.** The mid-size squid hunter of the Monte San Giorgio basin — a fast-ish tier between *Mixosaurus* and *Besanosaurus*, and a clean example of "same genus, different size classes" for a growth ladder.

Sources: Swiss ichthyosaurs review 2024 https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11366730/ ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cymbospondylus

### 5. Mixosaurus cornalianus

**Species / age / locality.** *Mixosaurus cornalianus* (Bassani 1886). Besano Formation (Grenzbitumenzone), Anisian–Ladinian boundary (~242 Ma), Monte San Giorgio (Switzerland) and Besano (Italy). Extremely abundant: >300 specimens in Zurich alone; the only Triassic ichthyosaur known from fully articulated skeletons. UNESCO site.

**Adult length.** Most specimens 0.7–1 m; adults typically under 1.5 m; the largest over 2 m (Wikipedia; Swiss review).

**Distinctive anatomy.**
- Dorsal fin — the oldest known in any amniote — preserved as soft tissue stiffened by fibre bundles (Renesto et al. 2020).
- Caudal fin with a well-developed triangular dorsal lobe of connective tissue over a long, low tail — not the post-Triassic lunate fluke.
- Heterodont dentition: pointed anterior teeth, blunter posterior crushing teeth.
- Forelimbs longer than hindlimbs, five digits, hyperphalangy.
- Large skull for the body; slender rostrum; scaleless skin.

**Behaviour.**
- Feeding: cephalopod hooklets in most gut contents; two specimens with small-fish scales and tiny vertebrae (**supported**). Crushing rear teeth suggest occasional hard prey (**inferred**).
- Locomotion: dorsal fin plus tail lobe → stable, sustained undulatory swimming; more "fish-like" than its basal relatives (**inferred**, Renesto 2020).
- Reproduction: viviparous; in situ embryos with variable orientation (head- or tail-first) but a very small sample (**supported**, low n). Viviparity is universal in Ichthyopterygia as far as known — no egg-laying ichthyosaur has ever been found.
- Diving: no direct data (**speculative**).

**Fun to play.** The starter/hatchling-tier ichthyosaur: small, agile, numerous, in a well-mapped biome with known co-inhabitants (*Besanosaurus*, *Cymbospondylus buchseri*, *Tanystropheus*). A visible dorsal fin is a strong silhouette for a 1 m body and rewards good soft-tissue art.

Sources: Renesto et al. 2020, *Acta Palaeontologica Polonica* https://www.app.pan.pl/article/item/app007312020.html ; Swiss review https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11366730/ ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mixosaurus

### 6. Cartorhynchus lenticarpus

**Species / age / locality.** *Cartorhynchus lenticarpus* Motani et al. 2014 (*Nature*). Nanlinghu Formation, Spathian, Early Triassic (~248 Ma), Majiashan, Chaohu, Anhui, China. One nearly complete skeleton; the most basal ichthyosauriform.

**Adult length.** Preserved 21.4 cm; ~40 cm reconstructed; mass ~2 kg (original) or as low as ~0.24 kg (later estimate). Ontogenetic stage uncertain.

**Distinctive anatomy.**
- Very short snout (about half skull length) with edentulous tips — first ichthyosauriform without the long rostrum.
- Large, flexible, poorly ossified "flipper-wrists" with extensive cartilage — could bend like a sea-turtle's flipper.
- Thick, pachyostotic ribs (ballast for shallow water).
- Short trunk, few vertebrae, small body.
- Large, robust hyoid (ceratohyal).
- Hidden dentition: CT (Huang et al. 2020, *Sci Rep*) revealed rounded, molariform teeth angled nearly perpendicular to the jaw, invisible in side view, in three rows per ramus.

**Behaviour.**
- Amphibious: flexible wrists, heavy ribs, short body → hauled out like seals/turtles (**inferred**, Motani 2014).
- Suction feeding: short snout + big hyoid (**inferred**); durophagy on shelled invertebrates from the molariform teeth (**supported**, Huang et al. 2020); both can coexist.
- Locomotion: anguilliform at slow speed, paddle-assisted (**inferred**).
- Reproduction: unknown; a land-going form could conceivably have laid eggs, but *Chaohusaurus* from the same beds was viviparous (**speculative**).

**Fun to play.** The one ichthyosaur-line animal that can crawl onto the shore — a genuine haul-out mechanic (the game's `airborne`/shore logic), plus a crunch-shellfish feeding mode. Tiny, so a hatchling/prey-tier body with a unique traversal trait.

Sources: Motani et al. 2014, *Nature* https://doi.org/10.1038/nature13866 ; Huang et al. 2020, *Scientific Reports* https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-020-64854-z ; UC Davis https://www.ucdavis.edu/blog/earliest-ichthyosaur-munched-shellfish ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cartorhynchus

### 7. Chaohusaurus / Utatsusaurus (brief)

**Species / age / locality.** *Chaohusaurus geishanensis* (Young & Dong 1972) and congeners — Nanlinghu Formation, Spathian (~248 Ma), Chaohu, Anhui, China. *Utatsusaurus hataii* Shikama et al. 1978 — Osawa Formation, Spathian, Utatsu-cho, Miyagi, Japan (also British Columbia).

**Adult length.** *Chaohusaurus* ~0.7–1.8 m depending on species; *Utatsusaurus* ~2.5–3 m, ~58 kg (Wikipedia).

**Distinctive anatomy.**
- Both: no dorsal fin, long low tail (no fluke), broad skull with gently tapering snout, five-digit paddles with hyperphalangy.
- *Utatsusaurus*: ~40 cylindrical presacral vertebrae; teeth in a primitive groove rather than sockets.
- *Chaohusaurus*: lizard-like proportions, small skull, tapered tail; the oldest ichthyopterygian with embryos.

**Behaviour.**
- Reproduction: Motani et al. (2014, *PLOS ONE*) describe a *Chaohusaurus* mother with three offspring — one inside, one exiting the pelvis **head-first**, one outside. Head-first birth (versus tail-first in later ichthyosaurs and whales) implies viviparity was inherited from terrestrial ancestors, not evolved at sea (**supported** specimen; **inferred** origin argument).
- Locomotion: eel-like anguilliform undulation, paddles for steering (**inferred**).
- Feeding: small fish and soft invertebrates (**inferred**, teeth).

**Fun to play.** The "first swimmers": lizard-bodied, tail-driven, born live in the shallows. A head-first-birth animation would be a strikingly specific and citable detail for a nursery scene.

Sources: Motani et al. 2014, *PLOS ONE* https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0088640 ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Utatsusaurus ; Sci.News https://www.sci.news/paleontology/science-chaohusaurus-fossil-live-reptile-birth-01763.html

### 8. Hupehsuchus nanchangensis

**Species / age / locality.** *Hupehsuchus nanchangensis* Young & Dong 1972. Jialingjiang Formation, Olenekian (Spathian), Early Triassic (~248 Ma), Hubei, China. Order Hupehsuchia — an ichthyosauromorph sister-group, not an ichthyosaur.

**Adult length.** ~1 m (Wikipedia; Fang et al. 2023 describe two new near-complete specimens).

**Distinctive anatomy.**
- Rows of dorsal osteoderm plates over the back, giving a crocodile-like armoured ridge (heavier than in *Nanchangosaurus*).
- Bipartite (split) neural spines under the plates.
- Long, narrow, toothless snout with an unfused upper jaw and grooves along the jaw margins.
- Basket-like gastralia; polydactyly (extra digits) in the hand.
- Elongate neck and comparatively small head; short paddles.

**Behaviour.**
- Filter feeding (Fang et al. 2023, *BMC Ecol Evol*): skull shape converges on baleen whales, with soft-tissue grooves read as baleen-like keratin and an enlarged buccal cavity → continuous ram filter feeding like a bowhead (**speculative/contested**).
- Re-examination (Motani, Pyenson & Jiang 2025, *PeerJ*): no intraoral space for baleen, the grooves are ambiguous in one specimen, the morphometrics were flawed, and a kg-scale animal cannot energetically afford balaenid filtering; skull is closest to a **pelican** — gulping/lunge feeding without a filter (**inferred**, currently the stronger case).
- Locomotion: stiff armoured trunk, undulation of the tail; slow (**inferred**).
- Reproduction: unknown (**speculative**).

**Fun to play.** An armoured, toothless gulper — a "pelican mouth" pouch-feeder on fish shoals is a distinctive feeding animation, and the dorsal plates give it a defensive stat and a silhouette unlike anything else in the roster.

Sources: Fang et al. 2023, *BMC Ecology and Evolution* https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s12862-023-02143-9 ; Motani, Pyenson & Jiang 2025, *PeerJ* https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12232927/ ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hupehsuchus

### 9. Guanlingsaurus / Guizhouichthyosaurus (brief)

**Species / age / locality.** *Guanlingsaurus liangae* Yin et al. 2000 and *Guizhouichthyosaurus tangae* Cao & Luo 2000 — both Xiaowa (= Wayao/Falang) Formation, Carnian (~235–227 Ma), Guanling, Guizhou, China. *Guanlingsaurus* was sunk into *Shastasaurus* (Sander 2011) and resurrected by Ji et al. (2013).

**Adult length.** *Guanlingsaurus* up to 8.3 m (juvenile 3.74 m). *Guizhouichthyosaurus* 4.8–6 m, some ~7 m, ~0.9–1.4 t.

**Distinctive anatomy.**
- *Guanlingsaurus*: wide triangular skull, very short snout (skull only 8–9% of body length), completely toothless with no dental groove.
- *Guizhouichthyosaurus*: long powerful snout with small conical teeth lacking cutting edges; sickle-shaped scapula; ~170 caudal vertebrae with an abrupt down-bend near the tip.
- Both: long slender bodies, no evidence of a dorsal fin.

**Behaviour.**
- *Guanlingsaurus* feeding: suction proposed (Sander 2011); Ji et al. 2013 found the hyoid too short — not a suction feeder (**contested**).
- *Guizhouichthyosaurus* feeding: Jiang et al. (2020, *iScience*) found a ~4 m thalattosaur (*Xinpusaurus xingyiensis*) in the stomach of a ~5 m individual — the oldest known megapredation by a marine tetrapod; limbs detached before the tail argues for predation, not scavenging (**supported**). Small teeth → grip-and-drown, then twist to tear (**inferred**).
- Locomotion: anguilliform (**inferred**).
- Reproduction: viviparous (**inferred**).

**Fun to play.** *Guizhouichthyosaurus* is the model for the game's "hold, drown, tear" grip mechanic: an apex that swallows prey almost its own length. *Guanlingsaurus* is its toothless neighbour — a good pairing for one biome.

Sources: Jiang et al. 2020, *iScience* https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2589004220305344 ; Sci.News https://www.sci.news/paleontology/thalattosaur-xinpusaurus-xingyiensis-stomach-triassic-ichthyosaur-guizhouichthyosaurus-08774.html ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guanlingsaurus ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guizhouichthyosaurus

Confidence labels: **supported** (direct fossil/analytical evidence), **inferred** (functional-morphology reasoning), **speculative**.

### 1. Tanystropheus (T. hydroides, T. longobardicus)

**Classification.** A tanystropheid archosauromorph — closer to crocodiles and dinosaurs than to lizards, but *not* a dinosaur.
**Age & locality.** Middle Triassic (Anisian–Ladinian) Besano Formation / Grenzbitumenzone, Monte San Giorgio (Switzerland/Italy); also Germany, Poland, China, and one Carnian Nova Scotia specimen.
**Size.** *T. hydroides*: ~5.25 m for the largest specimens, often rounded to ~6 m; *T. longobardicus*: absolute maximum ~2 m (Spiekman et al. 2020; Wikipedia). Neck ≈ trunk + tail combined, so ~3 m on the big species.

**Anatomy.**
- Neck of only **13 hyperelongate cervicals**; each cervical rib runs back as a strut up to 3× its vertebra, overlapping several joints and bracing the neck's underside.
- Skull 138 mm in the CT'd *T. hydroides* (PIMUZ T 2790): nostrils set high/dorsally on a crocodile-like snout, an interlocking "fish-trap" of long recurved fangs at the front, a row of large recurved vomerine teeth on the palate.
- *T. longobardicus* has tricuspid cheek teeth (small shelled/soft prey); *T. hydroides* single-cusped — two species, not growth stages (bone histology confirms adults of the small form).
- Long, muscular hindlimbs and tail base; forelimbs short; no paddles, no tail fin, no pachyostosis of the kind seen in nothosaurs.
- Only 13 cervicals but 32 in *Dinocephalosaurus* — convergent long necks by different routes.

**Neck flexibility.** Renesto & Saller (2018) and Spiekman et al. (2020) agree the neck was **stiff**: overlapping cervical-rib bundles blocked ventral flexion, horizontally overlapping zygapophyses limited lateral bending, and the low neural spines gave little dorsal muscle leverage. Best reading: a nearly rigid beam that could be swung as a unit from the base and raised a little (dorsal plane), with meaningful lateral or vertical *curling* essentially impossible (**inferred**). The strike was a **lateral snap of the skull**, not a swan-like neck lunge (Spiekman et al. 2020, PeerJ 2020) (**inferred, strong**).

**Shore-fishing vs swimming.** Renesto & Saller (2018) argued shore-dwelling/semi-aquatic: land-capable limbs, hindlimb-driven bursts of swimming in shallows, lightweight tendon-stabilised neck. Spiekman et al. (2020) shifted the balance toward **aquatic**: dorsal nostrils, plesiosaur-like fang trap, and skull proportions only make sense underwater, while the body shows *no* fast-swimming adaptations — so a **bottom-lurking aquatic ambush predator** in shallow water, using the neck to keep the bulky body far from wary prey (**inferred, current consensus**). Terrestrial excursions remain possible (**speculative**). Diet: fish and cephalopods (large sp.), small shelled animals/shrimp (small sp.) (**inferred**). Reproduction: unknown; presumed egg-laying on land (**speculative**).

**Decapitation (Spiekman & Mujal 2023, Current Biology).** Two Monte San Giorgio specimens — one of each species — preserve only skull + anterior neck, ending abruptly with bite traces at the break: the neck was **bitten clean through** and the body eaten, head left. Candidate predators: a nothosaur or ichthyosaur for the big one, a large fish or another marine reptile for the small (**supported**).

**Gameplay.** A stiff 3 m neck that sweeps as one rigid boom, with a fast sideways head-snap and a huge reach-to-body ratio, is a great ambush kit — and the neck is the canonical weak point: a predator that gets a bite on the neck mid-way should be able to sever it outright. Poor top speed and no dive endurance force a lurk-and-strike style.

Sources: [Spiekman et al. 2020, Current Biology](https://www.cell.com/current-biology/fulltext/S0960-9822(20)31017-4); [Spiekman et al. 2020 PeerJ skull CT](https://peerj.com/articles/10299/); [Spiekman & Mujal 2023, Current Biology](https://www.cell.com/current-biology/fulltext/S0960-9822(23)00475-X); [Renesto & Saller 2018](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/323723699_Evidences_for_a_semi_aquatic_life_style_in_the_triassic_diapsid_reptile_tanystropheus); [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tanystropheus); [Sci.News on decapitation](https://www.sci.news/paleontology/tanystropheus-predators-12015.html).

### 2. Dinocephalosaurus orientalis

**Age & locality.** Upper Guanling Formation, late Anisian (Pelsonian), ~244 Ma; Xinmin (Guizhou) and Luoping (Yunnan), SW China. Non-archosauriform archosauromorph.
**Size.** Postcranial skeleton "as much as 6 m"; the commonly quoted ~5 m is a mid-range figure. Neck ~2.3 m, more than twice the trunk (Spiekman et al. 2024).

**Anatomy.**
- **32 cervical vertebrae** (vs 13 in *Tanystropheus*), each moderately elongate, with cervical ribs forming a stiffening ventral bundle.
- Low, narrow skull with a single premaxillary fang and two maxillary fangs; heterodont teeth with striated enamel and plicidentine (folded dentine) — first known in an archosauromorph.
- Paddle-shaped hands and feet with only 6 carpals and 3 tarsals ossified (paedomorphic, as in nothosaurs); limbs useless on land.
- Long tail; overall body plan convergent on long-necked plesiosaurs.
- Embryo preserved inside the ribcage facing forward (not swallowed prey).

**Behaviour.** Fully marine, never left the water (**supported** by limbs + viviparity). **Viviparous** — first archosauromorph known to give live birth (Liu et al. 2017, Nat. Commun.) (**supported**). Fed on small fish, likely swallowed head-first; fang cage for gripping slippery prey (**inferred**). Suction feeding via neck-rib expansion was proposed (2004) but is now doubted (**speculative**). Swimming: undulation of a long body with limb paddling; not a fast pursuit predator (**inferred**). Neck function: keeping the body out of prey's view, plesiosaur-style (**inferred**).

**Gameplay.** Thirty-two neck joints allow a genuinely snaky, reach-around-corners neck — very different feel from *Tanystropheus*'s rigid boom. As a hazard, a head that appears from behind a rock while the body is two body-lengths away is a classic jump-scare.

Sources: [Spiekman et al. 2024, EESTRSE](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/earth-and-environmental-science-transactions-of-royal-society-of-edinburgh/article/dinocephalosaurus-orientalis-li-2003-a-remarkable-marine-archosauromorph-from-the-middle-triassic-of-southwestern-china/C7D48539139475EFCAAC35342089ACB8); [Liu et al. 2017, Nature Communications](https://www.nature.com/articles/ncomms14445); [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dinocephalosaurus); [Phys.org 2024](https://phys.org/news/2024-02-paleontologists-million-year-chinese-dragon.html).

### 3. Askeptosaurus italicus

**Age & locality.** Middle Triassic (late Anisian) Besano Formation/Grenzbitumenzone, Monte San Giorgio. An askeptosaurid thalattosaur.
**Size.** ~2–3 m total (Müller 2005; thalattosaur review 2024); skull ~26 cm.

**Anatomy.**
- Long, slender, straight snout (not downturned like thalattosauroids); low, broad-backed skull.
- Small, sharp, slightly recurved teeth in shallow sockets: up to 12 premaxillary, 16 maxillary.
- Elongate neck (13 cervicals), 25 dorsals, **60+ caudals** — a very long, laterally compressed tail.
- Small but complete limbs, five clawed digits; paddle-like hands/feet; twisted humerus.
- Plesiomorphic braincase; unique fronto-lacrimal contact.

**Behaviour.** Axial (anguilliform) tail-driven swimmer; limbs for steering, possibly capable of hauling out (**inferred**). Piscivore/cephalopod-eater given the needle teeth and slender snout (**inferred**). Air-breathing, shallow-water, probably not a deep diver (**inferred**). Reproduction unknown (**speculative**; egg-laying if it could haul out, but no evidence).

**Gameplay.** An eel-like body of 3 m that turns in its own length: a mid-tier fish-chaser with a long, flexible tail whip; sharing the Monte San Giorgio lagoon with *Tanystropheus* gives two contrasting long-necked silhouettes.

Sources: [Müller 2005, Can. J. Earth Sci.](https://cdnsciencepub.com/doi/10.1139/e05-030); [Thalattosauria review 2024 (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11427521/); [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Askeptosaurus).

### 4. Xinpusaurus and Anshunsaurus (brief)

**Age & locality.** Xiaowa (Wayao) Formation, Carnian, Guanling, Guizhou — the most diverse thalattosaur fauna known (four genera, six species). *Anshunsaurus* also from the Ladinian Zhuganpo Member (Xingyi).
**Size.** *Anshunsaurus huangguoshuensis* ~3.5 m (largest thalattosaur; tail ≥ half the body). *Xinpusaurus suni* smaller — ~1.3 m typical; the review gives 2–3 m for the genus (flagged: sources disagree).

**Anatomy — Xinpusaurus.** Short neck; upturned premaxilla/maxilla; **pointed front teeth + broad crushing back teeth** (durophagous); massive quadrate; radius with convex outer edge. **Anshunsaurus.** Long neck, slender straight skull, tiny limbs, long paddle tail; elongated jugal, fused postorbital–postfrontal.

**Behaviour.** Both tail-undulating swimmers (**inferred**). *Xinpusaurus* crushed shelled prey (bivalves, crustaceans) (**inferred, strong**); *Anshunsaurus* fish-eater (**inferred**). Reproduction unknown.

**Gameplay.** A durophage vs a piscivore in the same shallow basin — the crab-cracker is slow and armoured-jawed, the fish-eater fast and fragile; a nice pair for niche-partitioned AI.

Sources: [Thalattosauria review 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11427521/); [Wikipedia Xinpusaurus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xinpusaurus); [Wikipedia Anshunsaurus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anshunsaurus); [Guanling black shales, Palaeontology 2008](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/j.1475-4983.2007.00735.x).

### 5. Atopodentatus unicus

**Age & locality.** Guanling Formation (Anisian, ~244 Ma), Daaozi village, Luoping, Yunnan. Affinities uncertain (sauropterygian-adjacent).
**Size.** ~2.75–3 m (Chun et al. 2016; Wikipedia).

**Anatomy.**
- Skull a **hammerhead**: premaxillae and dentaries expanded sideways into a broad T-shaped bar.
- Two tooth types — chisel/peg-like teeth along the front edge of the bar, and dense needle-like teeth further back forming a mesh.
- The 2014 "zipper smile" (a vertical median split of the snout) was a crushing artefact; 2016 specimens fixed it.
- Long body, short neck, robust limbs with clawed digits; tail moderately long.
- Small orbits.

**Behaviour.** **Earliest herbivorous marine reptile**: scraped algae off the seafloor with the front chisels, then expelled water through the needle-tooth sieve, keeping the plant matter — a two-stage scrape-and-filter (**inferred, strong**; Chun, Rieppel, Cheng & Fraser 2016). Slow bottom-grazing swimmer, paddling with limbs; robust limbs suggest it could haul onto tidal flats (**speculative**). Reproduction unknown.

**Gameplay.** The one Triassic marine grazer — an ideal peaceful "cow" that mows biofilm off rocks and ignores the player unless bitten; its hammerhead is instantly recognisable and the filter-feed animation (mouth open, water jet out the sides) is a set-piece.

Sources: [Chun et al. 2016, Science Advances](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/sciadv.1501659); [Sci.News](https://www.sci.news/paleontology/atopodentatus-unicus-earliest-herbivorous-marine-reptile-03848.html); [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atopodentatus).

### 6. Odontochelys semitestacea

**Age & locality.** Xiaowa/Wayao Formation, Carnian (~220 Ma, sometimes 228), Guanling, Guizhou — Nanpanjiang Trough, a shallow marine basin ringed by land. Stem turtle (Li et al. 2008, Nature).
**Size.** ~40 cm total (Wikipedia; holotype scale).

**Anatomy.**
- **Teeth in both jaws** (no beak).
- **Complete plastron, no carapace**: dorsal ribs broadened but unfused, neural plates only — the state of an embryonic turtle.
- Long tail (longer than any later turtle), elongate snout, scapula without an acromion.
- Limbs unspecialised; no paddles.

**Behaviour.** Nearshore marine, supported by the depositional setting and isotopes (**supported/inferred**); a minority view argues a terrestrial/freshwater burrower whose carcass washed out (**speculative**). Bottom-walking or slow paddler (**inferred**). Diet: the pointed teeth suggest small invertebrates or omnivory; some argue herbivory (**speculative**). A belly-first shell is consistent with protection against attack from below in open water (**inferred**). Egg-laying on land presumed (**speculative**).

**Gameplay.** A tiny, toothed, half-shelled turtle: armoured from below, soft on top — an ambush from above beats it, an attack from beneath bounces off. Good small prey with a twist.

Sources: [Li et al. 2008, Nature](https://www.nature.com/articles/nature07533); [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Odontochelys); [Scientific American](https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/new-fossil-origin-of-the-turtle-discovered/).

### 7. Aphaneramma rostratum

**Age & locality.** Early Triassic (Smithian/early Olenekian) "Fish Niveau", Lusitaniadalen Member, Vikinghøgda Formation, Spitsbergen (Svalbard); congeners in Pakistan, Madagascar, Russia, W. Australia. Lonchorhynchine trematosaurid temnospondyl — an amphibian.
**Size.** Skull ~40 cm (Wikipedia); Svalbard growth series give postnareal skull lengths 97–375 mm with the snout tip unrecorded, so big skulls exceeded 50 cm. Total length is not published; at trematosaur proportions ~1.5–2 m (**flagged estimate**).

**Anatomy.**
- Extreme gharial-like snout, among the longest-snouted tetrapods ever.
- Small, numerous marginal teeth plus palatal tusks.
- Lateral-line grooves on the skull roof (aquatic sensory canals).
- Eyes set far back; flat, low skull.
- Long, shallow body with a laterally compressed swimming tail; small limbs.

**Behaviour.** **Marine**: the most abundant of eight temnospondyls in a nearshore shelf deposit; coprolites full of fish scales and bone; the group vanished when the water went deep and hypoxic (**supported**). Fish-snatcher using rapid sideways jaw swipes (**inferred, strong**). Lateral-line-guided ambush in murky water (**inferred**). Reproduction: amphibian larvae are unknown for trematosaurs; whether they bred in seawater or estuaries is open (**speculative**).

**Gameplay.** A crocodile-shaped amphibian in salt water is a novelty; the sideways jaw-swipe is a distinct attack from a straight lunge, and lateral-line sensing could let it track a player it cannot see.

Sources: [NHM Oslo Svalbard temnospondyls](https://www.nhm.uio.no/english/research/projects/temnospondyl/); [Tetrapod Zoology on trematosauroids](https://www.scientificamerican.com/blog/tetrapod-zoology/trematosauroid-temnospondyls/); [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aphaneramma).

### 8. Helveticosaurus zollingeri and Eusaurosphargis dalsassoi (brief)

**Helveticosaurus.** Besano Formation, Monte San Giorgio (Anisian/Ladinian, ~242 Ma). Preserved skeleton 2–2.5 m; ~3.6 m with full tail (Wikipedia). Short, blunt ~25 cm skull with long **caniniform fangs**; hyperphalangy in long slender fingers; long muscular tail. Affinities unresolved (once a placodont; now a basal marine diapsid, sister to *Eusaurosphargis*). Behaviour: combined tail undulation with forelimb paddling (**inferred**); fangs for soft prey — cephalopods, fish (**inferred**).

**Eusaurosphargis.** Besano and Prosanto Formations plus Winterswijk (Netherlands), Anisian/Ladinian. The 2017 Prosanto juvenile is ~20 cm; adult size unknown. Extensive **osteoderm armour** in rows down the back, flanks and limbs; limbs built for land. Scheyer et al. (2017) concluded it was **not aquatic** — a shore-dwelling armoured reptile washed into the basin (**inferred**), semi-aquatic not excluded.

**Gameplay.** *Helveticosaurus* is a mid-size fanged bruiser without a clear analogue; *Eusaurosphargis* is a spiky little shore-lizard that a player could meet only in the shallows or find as carrion.

Sources: [Scheyer et al. 2017, Sci. Rep.](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-017-04514-x); [Wikipedia Helveticosaurus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Helveticosaurus); [Wikipedia Eusaurosphargis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eusaurosphargis).

### 9. Phytosaurs as coastal Late Triassic predators (brief)

**Representative.** *Mystriosuchus planirostris* (Norian, Germany; also a complete ~4 m skeleton from the marine Calcare di Zorzino, Endenna, Lombardy — Gozzi & Renesto 2003) and *M. steinbergeri* (Norian Dachstein Limestone, Austria: at least four ~4 m individuals from a **marine lagoon**, Butler et al. 2019). *Smilosuchus* (Chinle, Arizona) is a large freshwater form up to ~7 m and not marine-associated.

**Anatomy.** Slender gharial-like snout; nostrils raised on a crest just in front of the eyes (crocodile-like surfacing without showing the head); ~74 caudals — tail 51% of body length; shorter, more paddle-like limbs than other phytosaurs; dorsal and ventral osteoderm armour. Archosauriform, not a crocodile.

**Behaviour.** Fish/soft-invertebrate diet (microwear) (**inferred, strong**). The Austrian and Italian occurrences are the best evidence that some phytosaurs lived in marine lagoons, not just washed in (**supported**). Tail-sculling ambusher at the surface; egg-layer on shore (**inferred/speculative**).

**Gameplay.** A surface-lurking crocodile analogue for the shoreline band: dangerous at the surface and in shallows, slow in open water.

Sources: [Butler et al. 2019, Zool. J. Linn. Soc.](https://academic.oup.com/zoolinnean/article-abstract/187/1/198/5487160); [Gozzi & Renesto 2003, RIPS](https://riviste.unimi.it/index.php/RIPS/article/view/5518); [Wikipedia Mystriosuchus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mystriosuchus).

### 10. Nanchangosaurus and Saurosphargis (brief)

**Nanchangosaurus suni.** Hupehsuchian; Jialingjiang Formation (Spathian, latest Early Triassic), Hubei. Total length just over 40 cm (Chen et al. 2014, PLOS One). Fusiform body, long broad **toothless** snout, single row of dermal ossicles above the neural spines, tiny weak forelimbs, rigid rib-and-gastralia basket. Behaviour: probed for soft invertebrates or small fish (**inferred**); tail-driven swimming with a stiff trunk (**inferred**). Sister group to ichthyosaurs.

**Saurosphargis volzi.** Saurosphargid; Anisian (~246 Ma), Gogolin, Poland (holotype destroyed in WWII) and Winterswijk. Twelve dorsals with **broadened ribs forming a closed rib cage** plus dorsal osteoderms — a turtle-like, pseudo-shelled body. Length unknown from the fragment; relatives ~1 m. Behaviour: slow, armoured bottom-dweller in shallow seas (**inferred**).

**Gameplay.** Both are small armoured slow-movers — good low-tier prey with a defensive twist (armour top, soft belly).

Sources: [Chen et al. 2014, PLOS One](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0102361); [Wikipedia Nanchangosaurus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nanchangosaurus); [Wikipedia Saurosphargis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saurosphargis); [Saurosphargiformes 2022 (PMC)](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9288826/).

### 11. Macrocnemus (brief)

**Species.** *M. bassanii* (Besano Fm. & Meride Limestone, Monte San Giorgio), *M. obristi* (Prosanto Fm., Swiss Alps), *M. fuyuanensis* (Zhuganpo Fm., Yunnan). Middle Triassic (Anisian–Ladinian). Tanystropheid archosauromorph, close relative of *Tanystropheus*.
**Size.** ~1.1–1.2 m; *M. bassanii* adults ~90 cm, juveniles 30 cm (Wikipedia; Miedema et al. 2020).

**Anatomy.** Small head on a slender but not hyper-long neck; **very long hindlimbs** with a hooked fifth metatarsal; long tail (52–53 caudals); short fifth toe (unlike *Tanystropheus*); U-shaped frontoparietal suture.

**Behaviour.** **Terrestrial**, coastal; capable of fast, possibly bipedal, running like a modern basilisk (**inferred, strong**); found in marine sediments only by wash-in. Insectivore/small-prey hunter (**inferred**). Egg-layer (**speculative**).

**Gameplay.** A shoreline runner glimpsed on rocks and beaches that dashes off when approached — pure ambience for a swimming player, or a rare snack if it wades.

Sources: [Miedema et al. 2020, PeerJ/PMC](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7381672/); [Fraser & Furrer 2013, Swiss J. Geosci.](https://sjg.springeropen.com/articles/10.1007/s00015-013-0137-5); [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Macrocnemus).

### PART A — Fish and invertebrates

#### 1. Helicoprion (and the Triassic eugeneodont question)

**Representative species:** *Helicoprion davisii* (Phosphoria Fm, Idaho; most common), *H. bessonowi* (Russia), *H. ergassaminon* (Idaho only).
**Age & locality:** **Early–Middle Permian only: Artinskian to Roadian, ~290–268 Ma** (Tapanila & Pruitt 2013 species revision). Idaho, Urals, Kazakhstan, Mexico, Japan, Australia. Claims of Late Carboniferous or Early Triassic *Helicoprion* on some wiki pages are **not** supported by the peer-reviewed record; treat the genus as strictly Permian. Do not put it in a Triassic sea.
**Triassic eugeneodont survivors (real):** caseodontids *Fadenia uroclasmato* and *Caseodus varidentis* from the Olenekian Sulphur Mountain Fm, Wapiti Lake, BC (Mutter & Neuman 2008), and *Fadenia* from the Early Triassic of East Greenland. These have a low symphyseal tooth arch rather than a spiral whorl, fusiform bodies, a lunate tail, no pelvic fins; ~1–2 m (inferred). If the game wants a "buzzsaw" in the Triassic, *Fadenia*/*Caseodus* is the honest stand-in.
**Adult length (Helicoprion):** whorls 35–40 cm across → 3–4 m; largest whorls → ~7–7.6 m (Tapanila et al. 2013 / Tapanila & Pruitt).
**Anatomy:** (1) single spiral whorl of ~130+ serrated crowns seated in the lower-jaw symphysis, occupying the whole mandibular arch (CT of IMNH 37899); (2) no teeth in the upper jaw — the whorl bites against a cartilage pad; (3) jaw-closing rotates the whorl backwards so teeth slice and rake prey into the throat; (4) cartilaginous skeleton, stem-holocephalan (Euchondrocephali), not a shark; (5) fusiform body, lunate tail, no pelvic fins (by eugeneodont analogy).
**Behaviour:** soft-prey (cephalopod/fish) slicer — **supported** (jaw mechanics); open-water cruiser — **inferred**; suction-assisted feeding — **speculative**.
**Fun-to-play:** a bite that is a *saw*: hold to close the jaw and the whorl rotates, dragging held prey inward — a grip that shortens the target rather than crushing it.
**Sources:** https://royalsocietypublishing.org/rsbl/article/9/2/20130057/49396 ; https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3639784 ; https://www.researchgate.net/publication/277417042_Unraveling_Species_Concepts_for_the_Helicoprion_Tooth_Whorl ; https://sp.lyellcollection.org/content/295/1/9.abstract

#### 2. Saurichthys

**Representative species:** *S. curionii*, *S. macrocephalus* (Ladinian Meride Limestone, Monte San Giorgio); *S. costasquamosus*, *S. paucitrichus* (Besano Fm); *S. yunnanensis* (Luoping).
**Age & locality:** Early–Late Triassic worldwide (Spitsbergen, Madagascar, China, Alps, Nevada); the genus is the Triassic's signature needle-fish.
**Adult length:** most species 0.6–1 m; the largest ~1.5 m; *S. curionii* about half a metre (Kogan et al. 2015; Maxwell et al.).
**Anatomy:** (1) pike/needlefish body, long rostrum with jaws of equal length and a single row of pointed teeth; (2) dorsal and anal fins set far back, opposite each other, forming a rudder with the symmetrical caudal fin; (3) reduced squamation — longitudinal scale rows only (dorsal, ventral, lateral) rather than full cover, making the body light; (4) elongate, partly ossified vertebral column with many segments, giving a stiff body; (5) large eyes.
**Behaviour:** ambush with fast-start acceleration (flow-tank/CFD work shows pike-like low flow disturbance) — **supported**; poor sustained swimmer, unlike tuna — **inferred**; viviparous: litters of 12–16+ in *S. curionii*, 24 in *S. macrocephalus*, phosphatised embryos (Renesto & Stockar 2009; Maxwell et al. 2018) — **supported**.
**Fun-to-play:** a strike-from-stillness fish: hover motionless, then a one-shot dash whose hit box is the rostrum; stamina punishes chasing. Live-born young could be a spawn mechanic.
**Sources:** https://journals.biologists.com/bio/article/4/12/1715/1391 ; https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00015-009-1323-3 ; https://academic.oup.com/zoolinnean/article/173/4/887/2449842 ; https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/pala.12355

#### 3. Birgeria

**Representative species:** *B. stensioei* (Anisian–Ladinian Besano Fm, Monte San Giorgio/Besano); *B. aldingeri* (Early Triassic, Spitsbergen); *B. americana* (Smithian, Elko County, Nevada); *B. groenlandica* (Greenland).
**Age & locality:** Early Triassic to Late Triassic, cosmopolitan.
**Adult length:** typically >1 m; *B. americana* 1.72–1.85 m; a Spitsbergen individual >2 m (Romano et al. 2017; Romano & Brinkmann 2009).
**Anatomy:** (1) long jaws and a very wide gape with three size-classes of conical teeth (large fangs plus rows of smaller teeth); (2) largely unossified braincase, palatoquadrate in three separate ossifications; (3) scales almost entirely lost — a naked, tuna-shaped body; (4) forked, deeply cleft caudal fin; single dorsal set far back; (5) large head relative to body; a heavily built shoulder girdle.
**Behaviour:** open-water pursuit predator on fish, including *Saurichthys* — **inferred** (dentition, body form); apex predator of the Triassic actinopterygians alongside big *Saurichthys* — **supported** (size ranking); stomach contents scarce — feeding on cephalopods **speculative**.
**Fun-to-play:** the "tuna" of the roster — fast cruise, wide-gape lunge that can swallow mid-size prey whole; the counterpart to *Saurichthys*' ambush.
**Sources:** https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-paleontology/article/marine-early-triassic-actinopterygii-from-elko-county-nevada-usa-implications-for-the-smithian-equatorial-vertebrate-eclipse/6E362373D3CFD016D645AEAC2ED54776 ; https://www.schweizerbart.de//papers/njgpa/detail/252/59656/ ; https://www.researchgate.net/publication/292397824

#### 4. Hybodus and Triassic hybodont sharks

**Representative species:** *Hybodus* spp. (Muschelkalk and Keuper teeth/spines); *Acrodus lateralis* (common), *A. gaillardoti* (large); *Palaeobates angustissimus* (Muschelkalk); *Hybodus hauffianus* is Jurassic, not Triassic — avoid it as a name.
**Age & locality:** hybodonts span Late Permian–Cretaceous; the Muschelkalk (Anisian–Ladinian, Germanic Basin) is the classic Triassic occurrence. Also Early Triassic of Yunnan and Spitsbergen.
**Adult length:** *Hybodus* up to ~2 m; *Palaeobates* ~1 m; *Acrodus gaillardoti* large — a 30 cm dorsal fin spine is attributed to it (Muschelkalk Museum).
**Anatomy:** (1) two dorsal fins each fronted by a stout spine with longitudinal ridges and a double row of posterior denticles; (2) males carry one or two pairs of hook-shaped cephalic spines behind the orbit (clasping aids in mating); (3) *Hybodus* cusped, multi-cusp teeth; *Acrodus* low crushing pavement teeth with a crimped crown (durophagy on bivalves, gastropods, crustaceans); (4) amphistylic jaw suspension; (5) heterocercal tail, and calcified-cartilage vertebral column absent (notochord).
**Behaviour:** *Acrodus* crushing shellfish — **supported** (tooth wear/morphology); *Hybodus* generalist fish/cephalopod predator — **inferred**; spines as anti-predator defence — **inferred**; males gripping females with cephalic hooks — **inferred** from position and dimorphism.
**Fun-to-play:** two body plans under one family: a crusher (*Acrodus*) that can eat the armoured stuff no one else can, and a spined generalist whose dorsal spines punish biters.
**Sources:** https://www.muschelkalkmuseum.org/en/exhibition/fossil_gallery/fish/ ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hybodontiformes ; https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8121075/ ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Palaeobates

#### 5. Rebellatrix divaricerca

**Age & locality:** Early Triassic (Smithian–Spathian) Vega-Phroso Siltstone Member, Sulphur Mountain Fm, Wapiti Lake Provincial Park, BC (Wendruff & Wilson 2012, JVP 32(3):499–511).
**Adult length:** estimated ~1.3 m (Wendruff & Wilson; Wikipedia summary).
**Anatomy:** (1) deeply forked, tuna-like caudal fin — unique among coelacanths, which otherwise have a three-lobed paddle tail; (2) narrow caudal peduncle; (3) elongate, fusiform body rather than the deep chunky coelacanth outline; (4) lobed paired fins retained (it is still an actinistian); (5) family of its own, Rebellatricidae.
**Behaviour:** active fast pursuit swimmer — **inferred** (tail shape and peduncle by analogy with tuna and sharks); predator on fish in the open water after the end-Permian extinction — **inferred**; occupying a shark-like niche while sharks were scarce — **speculative**.
**Fun-to-play:** a coelacanth that breaks the rule — an unexpectedly fast lobe-fin. Give it top sustained speed but a clumsy turn; the lobed fins can hover. Same locality as *Fadenia*/*Caseodus*, so an Early Triassic BC biome could host both.
**Sources:** https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/02724634.2012.657317 ; https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2012/05/120502162441.htm ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rebellatrix

#### 6. Ceratites nodosus and Triassic ammonoids

**Representative species:** *Ceratites nodosus* (Upper Muschelkalk, Ladinian, Germany/France); *Tropites subbullatus* (Tuvalian, upper Carnian Hallstatt Limestone, Austria; index species); *Choristoceras marshi* (Rhaetian Kössen Fm, Austrian Alps; the last Triassic ammonoid zone).
**Adult length:** *C. nodosus* commonly 7–13 cm diameter, rare specimens ~20 cm; *Tropites* mostly 5–10 cm, involute and globose with a narrow umbilicus, coarse ribs ending in nodes beside a keel; *Choristoceras* small (<3 cm), an open, uncoiled crook with prominent ribbing.
**Anatomy:** (1) *ceratitic* suture — rounded saddles, saw-toothed lobes, intermediate between goniatitic and ammonitic; (2) discoidal, evolute shell with strong ribs and ventrolateral nodes (*Ceratites*); (3) chambered phragmocone with a siphuncle for buoyancy regulation, as in *Nautilus*; (4) heteromorph *Choristoceras*: an initially planispiral then straightened, hooked shell — a drifting/vertical-migrating form; (5) aptychus jaws in some Triassic groups.
**Behaviour:** neutral buoyancy by cameral gas/liquid exchange — **supported** (shell physics, *Nautilus* analogue); jet propulsion by funnel — **supported** by analogy; *Ceratites* a slow near-bottom scavenger/predator of the shallow Muschelkalk sea — **inferred**; heteromorphs as planktonic drifters — **inferred**.
**Fun-to-play:** a "buoyancy" body: hold to rise, release to sink, dash is a backwards jet you must aim with the shell behind you. Hard-shelled; a poor turner; a snack for placodonts.
**Sources:** https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ceratites ; https://www.muschelkalkmuseum.org/en/exhibition/fossil_gallery/cephalopods/ ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Choristoceratoidea ; https://www.thefossilforum.com/blogs/entry/184-the-tuvalian-substage-of-the-triassic-hallstatt-limestone-in-austria/

#### 7. Triassic coleoids: Aulacoceras, Atractites, Phragmoteuthis

**Representative species:** *Aulacoceras sulcatum* (Carnian–Norian Hallstatt facies, Austria); *Atractites* spp. (upper Anisian Wetterstein/Reifling limestones, Tyrol, Slovakia; type *A. breviconus*); *Phragmoteuthis bisinuata* (Carnian, Polzberg/Lunz, Lower Austria).
**Age & locality:** aulacocerids from the Early Triassic (rare) to Jurassic; *Phragmoteuthis* Late Triassic–Early Jurassic. The Polzberg Konservat-Lagerstätte (Carnian Pluvial Episode) is the prime soft-tissue site.
**Adult length:** *Phragmoteuthis* mantle+shell ~20–30 cm (inferred from Polzberg slabs); *Aulacoceras* rostra to 20 cm, whole animal perhaps 40–60 cm (inferred); *Atractites* small-to-medium.
**Anatomy:** (1) internal chambered phragmocone with a marginal siphuncle; (2) a solid calcitic rostrum (guard) behind it in aulacocerids, striated in *Aulacoceras*; (3) *Phragmoteuthis* has a broad three-lobed pro-ostracum extending beyond the phragmocone — an early squid-like sheet; (4) arm hooks (onychites) in pairs along the arms, preserved in Lunz specimens; (5) ink sac and ink preserved (grainy, 0.1–1 µm ultrastructure); gills preserved in rare specimens.
**Behaviour:** jet-propelled, ink-releasing, hook-armed grappler of small fish/crustaceans — **supported** (ink sac, hooks); mid-water schooling — **speculative**; predators on them: *Saurichthys* and marine reptiles (bromalites at Polzberg) — **supported**.
**Fun-to-play:** an ink cloud as a break-away move that also blinds pursuers; hooks as a grip that latches rather than bites.
**Sources:** https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1871174X07000601 ; https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8370992/ ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phragmoteuthis ; https://www.researchsquare.com/article/rs-3655162/v1

#### 8. Encrinus liliiformis and Traumatocrinus

**Encrinus liliiformis** — Upper Muschelkalk (Anisian–Ladinian) of Germany, Switzerland, Poland. Column up to ~150 cm, crown with 10 arms (pinnulate, close-packed) lifting the calyx above the seabed to filter plankton-rich currents. Lived in clusters attached to hardgrounds and to bivalve (terquemiid "false oyster") / crinoid-root bioherms; billions of shed columnals ("trochites") built the metre-thick crinoidal limestones of the Trochitenkalk Formation. Ecophenotypes vary with current (Hagdorn & co.). **Supported:** sessile filter feeding in current-swept shallow water; **inferred:** meadow density of many individuals per m².

**Traumatocrinus hsui** — Carnian Xiaowa Fm, Guanling, Guizhou (Wang et al. 2008; Hagdorn, Wang & Wang 2007). The first pseudoplanktonic crinoid: colonies of >150 individuals fixed by anastomosing root-cirri to driftwood logs (one log 3.3 m; stems in a colony to >11 m). Crinoids attached preferentially to the *ends* of the log; crowns of similar size but stems of different lengths, so the colony filtered a whole column of water below the raft. Crown: ten major arms branching to ~40 pinnulate arms, aboral spines at branching points; crown up to ~40 cm across. Stem construction lightened by pore systems so the log stayed afloat. When the log waterlogged and sank into the anoxic basin the whole colony was buried intact. Niche taken by *Seirocrinus*/*Pentacrinites* in the Norian. **Supported:** log attachment, colony structure; **inferred:** feeding by hanging crown-down in the current under the raft.
**Fun-to-play:** the log raft is a *mobile scenery prop* — a drifting shelter with a curtain of crinoids beneath it; a small player can hide among the stems; a big animal can shake the raft.
**Sources:** https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/j.1475-4983.2007.00735.x ; https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0031018206006080 ; https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s12542-016-0285-9 ; https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s13358-010-0007-y ; https://www.muschelkalkmuseum.org/en/exhibition/fossil_gallery/echinoderms/sea_lilies/

#### 9. Flat clams and Placunopsis bioherms (brief)

**Daonella** (Anisian–Ladinian), **Halobia** (Ladinian–Norian), **Monotis** (Norian–Rhaetian) — thin, flat, radially ribbed "paper clams" that pave black-shale and Hallstatt-type bedding planes in mono-specific shell beds (lenses to 30 cm, shells concave-up, no fragmentation). Palaeoecology: most likely reclining/byssally-attached epibenthos on soft, oxygen-poor floors, opportunists blooming in brief oxygenation events, possibly chemosymbiotic (Schatz 2005; Hopkin & McRoberts 2005); the older "pseudoplankton on logs" idea is now the minority view for *Daonella*/*Halobia*. Shell to ~5–8 cm (*Monotis salinaria* ~4–6 cm). Excellent index fossils. **Supported:** shell-bed density; **inferred:** dysoxic-floor lifestyle.
**Placunopsis ostracina** (Upper Muschelkalk): a 15 mm, 0.5 mm-thick cementing bivalve that encrusted hardgrounds and shells; stacked right valves built stromatolite-like mounds up to 4.5 m high and 10 m across — the first metazoan buildups after the end-Permian extinction, on the basinward shoulders of swells in eutrophic water, encrusted by spirorbid worms and forams and rooted by *Encrinus*.
**Prop note:** flat-clam pavements as a floor texture in the black-shale biome; *Placunopsis* mounds as low domes in the Muschelkalk.
**Sources:** https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0921818120300862 ; https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0031018204005577 ; https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/j.1475-4983.2008.00776.x ; https://placunopsis.univie.ac.at/ ; https://www.researchgate.net/publication/237715693

#### 10. Triassic crustaceans (brief)

**Thylacocephalans** — peak diversity in the Triassic. Bivalved shield enclosing the body, hypertrophied compound eyes, three pairs of large raptorial limbs (the last pair largest), 8–22 trunk segments. *Ankitokazocaris* (Olenekian, Madagascar; Osawa Fm, Japan) ~4 cm; *Austriocaris carinata/striata* (Carnian Polzberg); new taxa from the Luoping Biota and the Upper Triassic Scheiblingbach Lagerstätte. Nektonic/nektobenthic ambush predators — **inferred**.
**Decapods** — *Antrimpos* (penaeid shrimp; Triassic of France, Germany, Italy, Switzerland; *A. colettoi* Upper Triassic of Italy) and the aegerid *Acanthochirana triassica*; *Aeger* sensu stricto is best known from the Jurassic, with Triassic aegerids as relatives. Early lobsters (erymids, glypheids) appear in the Early–Middle Triassic (Paris Biota, Idaho; Luoping). Body ~5–15 cm. Bottom-walking, tail-flip escape — **supported** by extant analogy.
**Horseshoe crabs** — *Yunnanolimulus luopingensis* (Anisian Luoping, ~244 Ma), ~10 cm, with chelicerae, five walking-leg pairs, book gills, muscles and setae preserved; *Tachypleus*-like Keuper forms in Germany. Sediment-ploughing scavengers — **supported**.
**Fun-to-play:** small-rung bodies: a thylacocephalan as a darting eye-and-claws hatchling, a shrimp with the tail-flip trait already in the engine, a horseshoe crab that walks and digs.
**Sources:** https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1871174X26000661 ; https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5658423/ ; https://www.researchgate.net/publication/361116583 ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antrimpos ; https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-3-032-19777-1_20

## Environments

### PART B — Environments (biome briefs)

#### 11. Monte San Giorgio / Besano intraplatform basin

**Age:** late Anisian–Ladinian (~242 Ma; ash bed U-Pb 242.1 ± 0.6 Ma).
**Depth:** 30–100 m (some estimates to ~130 m), below storm wave base; a restricted intraplatform basin rimmed by carbonate platforms (Salvatore Dolomite), connected to open water only intermittently.
**Floor:** finely laminated black shale alternating with dolomite/limestone beds (Besano Fm, 16 m thick); euxinic-to-anoxic bottom water, **no benthic community** — no burrowers, no shells in life position, carcasses lie articulated. The floor is a flat, dark, lamination-textured mud with occasional fallen carcasses, fish, and drifted plant fragments and *Daonella* pavements. Thin cream/grey volcanic ash beds (bentonites) interrupt the black shale.
**Water column:** stratified — an oxygenated upper layer with fish, *Saurichthys*, *Birgeria*, ichthyosaurs (*Besanosaurus*, *Mixosaurus*), nothosaurs, pachypleurosaurs, *Tanystropheus*; a dead lower layer.
**Visibility/lighting:** dim blue-green; below ~40 m only a residual glow; near-black at the floor. Fine particle "snow".
**Sessile life:** essentially none on the floor; upslope, on the platform rim, dasyclad algae and small sponges.
**Props:** ash-bed banding in exposed slope; drifted conifer trunks; fish carcasses; *Daonella* shell pavements; ichthyosaur skeletons in the mud; rare land insects/scorpions blown in.
**Sources:** https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10912274/ ; https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00015-012-0093-5 ; https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00015-010-0008-2 ; https://peerj.com/articles/2234/

#### 12. Muschelkalk epicontinental sea

**Age:** Anisian–Ladinian, Central European Basin (Germany, Poland, France, Switzerland, Netherlands).
**Depth:** shallow — a few metres to a few tens of metres; storm- and wave-worked. Lower and Upper Muschelkalk marine; Middle Muschelkalk restricted, hypersaline (salt and gypsum).
**Floor:** carbonate mud, oolitic shoals ("Kornstein") on swells, hardgrounds encrusted by *Placunopsis*, crinoid gravel (Trochitenkalk), shell beds of terebratulids, *Coenothyris*, *Plagiostoma* and *Ceratites*; tempestite layers; marl "Tonplatten" in the basin centre.
**Sessile life:** *Encrinus* meadows (stems to 1.5 m), *Placunopsis*/terquemiid bioherms, oysters, serpulids, rare small corals; brachiopods.
**Mobile:** *Ceratites*, nautiloids (*Germanonautilus*), *Acrodus*/*Hybodus*/*Palaeobates*, colobodontids, *Saurichthys*, nothosaurs, placodonts (*Placodus*, *Cyamodus*).
**Visibility/lighting:** bright, sunlit, greenish-turbid; storms stir sediment.
**Shore:** the Grès à Voltzia delta (Vosges) — the conifer *Voltzia heterophylla* in dense stands, horsetails (*Equisetites*, *Schizoneura*), ferns, in brackish lagoons and ponds — is the model shoreline, with sabkha flats in the hypersaline phases.
**Props:** crinoid meadows, oolite ripples, *Placunopsis* domes, *Ceratites* shells on the floor, gypsum crusts in hypersaline shallows, Voltzia trunks and horsetail reed beds at the waterline.
**Sources:** https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0012825225000467 ; https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1631068305000497 ; https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7067256/ ; https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/dep2.59

#### 13. Wetterstein / Dachstein reefs and Late Triassic coral–sponge reefs

**Age & place:** Wetterstein Limestone — Ladinian–early Carnian, Northern Calcareous Alps, Carpathians; Dachstein Limestone — Norian–Rhaetian (Lofer cycles), with Rhaetian reefs at Steinplatte, Gosau, Adnet.
**Depth:** reef crest at 0–10 m; fore-reef slope to 50–100 m+; back-reef lagoon 1–20 m, tidal flats emergent (Lofer cycle members A/B/C).
**Builders:** Wetterstein reefs are mostly **calcareous sponges** (sphinctozoans, inozoans), *Tubiphytes* (a microproblematicum/microbe crust), microbialites and cement crusts, with corals minor. By the Norian–Rhaetian scleractinians dominate: *Retiophyllia* (= "Thecosmilia" of older literature) phaceloid thickets, *Astraeomorpha*, *Pinacophyllum*, still with calcisponges, *Tubiphytes*, hydrozoans, solenoporacean red algae, encrusting forams.
**Lagoon:** dasyclad green algae — *Diplopora annulata* meadows (Wetterstein) and *Heteroporella* (Dachstein), megalodont bivalves in life position, gastropods, *Thecosmilia* patch reefs, stromatolitic mats on the flats.
**Visibility/lighting:** clear, oligotrophic, bright; white carbonate sand; strong blue.
**Props:** phaceloid coral bushes (candelabra shape), vase sponges, *Diplopora* tufts, megalodont "cow-hoof" shells, patch reefs on white sand, spur-and-groove crest, fore-reef talus blocks, stromatolite flats.

#### 14. Early Triassic microbialites and the post-extinction sea; Gipskeuper sabkha lagoons

**Microbialites:** the first 1–2 conodont zones after the end-Permian extinction are the classic "Lilliput" interval — dwarfed gastropods, bivalves and forams (Twitchett 2007). Larger gastropods (to 70 mm) reappear by the Smithian, so the effect is short (Brayard et al. 2010). Four microbialite peaks span the Griesbachian to Spathian: stromatolites and thrombolites on shallow ramps (Virgin Limestone, Nevada; Perth Basin; South China), grown by cyanobacteria under high carbonate saturation, dysoxia and few grazers.
**Depth/floor:** 0–20 m; domal and columnar stromatolites, thrombolite mounds, ooid shoals, microgastropod-packed limestones; sparse burrowing; **visibility** bright but often turbid, water hot (~35–40 °C at the equator).
**Life:** microgastropods, *Claraia* and *Unionites* bivalves, lingulid brachiopods, ammonoids, *Saurichthys*, *Birgeria*, early ichthyosaurs (*Utatsusaurus*, *Chaohusaurus*), coelacanths (*Rebellatrix*), eugeneodonts.
**Gipskeuper (Carnian, SW Germany):** a playa/sabkha of brackish to hypersaline ponds that dried seasonally, gypsum and halite crusts, red mudflats — where *Henodus chelyops* (a 1 m, turtle-shaped placodont with baleen-like denticles) lived, the harshest habitat any sauropterygian took on. Floor: gypsum crystals, algal mats, desiccation cracks; **very** shallow (cm–2 m), warm, clear but salt-hazed.
**Props:** stromatolite domes, thrombolite heads, ooid ripples, gypsum rosettes, salt crusts, cracked mud, dwarf shell hash.

#### 15. Luoping / Panxian / Guanling biotas (South China)

**Setting:** intraplatform basins on the Yangtze Platform beside the Nanpanjiang Basin — restricted circulation, density-stratified water, dysoxic-to-anoxic bottoms.
**Luoping (Pelsonian, Anisian, ~244 Ma):** thinly laminated micritic limestone and silty limestone; a semi-enclosed lagoon-basin a few tens of metres deep; sea floor mostly quiet and dysoxic with episodes of oxygenation (moderate bioturbation, trace fossils), so a patchy benthos: sea urchins, crinoids, bivalves, gastropods, brachiopods, horseshoe crabs (*Yunnanolimulus*), lobsters, shrimps, thylacocephalans, mysids; surface and mid-water: *Saurichthys yunnanensis*, colobodontids (*Feroxichthys*), ichthyosaurs, nothosaurs, *Mixosaurus*; drifted conifer shoots and land insects. Stromatolites on adjacent shallows. High productivity aided preservation.
**Panxian (Pelsonian, Guanling Fm Member II):** similar dark laminated limestone; marine reptiles (*Mixosaurus*, *Nothosaurus*, *Placodus*) and fish; volcanic ash beds.
**Guanling (Carnian, Xiaowa Fm):** deeper, stagnant black shale/mudstone of a marginal depression; **no benthos**; the whole biota is pelagic — *Traumatocrinus* log rafts, giant ichthyosaurs (*Guizhouichthyosaurus*, *Guanlingichthyosaurus*), thalattosaurs (*Anshunsaurus*), *Halobia* pavements, ammonoids, driftwood.
**Visibility/lighting:** Luoping — moderate, greenish, sunlit at the surface; Guanling — dark, still, a lit surface with logs and crinoid curtains above a black floor.
**Props:** log rafts with hanging crinoids, *Halobia* pavements, ichthyosaur skeletons, horseshoe-crab trails, sea-urchin patches, drifted conifer branches.

#### 16. Shoreline plants visible from the water, and algae

- **Voltzia** (voltzialean conifer; *V. heterophylla*, Grès à Voltzia, Anisian, Vosges): bushy tree to ~10 m, short scale-like leaves on drooping shoots, cone clusters; dominant coastal tree of the Muschelkalk sea's western shore.
- **Pleuromeia** (lycopsid; Early–Middle Triassic, Laurasia and Gondwana): unbranched pole 1–2 m with a tuft of strap leaves and a single terminal cone on a four-lobed rhizomorph base; monodominant stands on coastal flats and salt marshes after the extinction — the classic "disaster flora".
- **Neocalamites / Equisetites** (horsetails): reed beds 2–5 m tall on wet margins and deltas throughout the Triassic; *Equisetites arenaceus* stems 10–20 cm across in the Keuper.
- **Cycads / Bjuvia**: *Bjuvia simplex* (Rhaetian, Scania) — a short-trunked cycad with large entire leaves; *Taeniopteris*-type foliage; cycads in Triassic wetlands.
- **Dicroidium** (corystosperm seed fern; Middle–Late Triassic Gondwana): forked fronds, shrubs to trees, the dominant Gondwanan lowland plant; with *Heidiphyllum* conifers.
- **Ginkgoaleans, Bennettitales (*Pterophyllum*), ferns (*Cladophlebis*)** round out coastal assemblages; *Ptilozamites*, *Lepacyclotes* in Laurasia.
- **Seaweed/algae:** no macroscopic fleshy seaweeds are reliably preserved; the calcified record is rich — dasyclad greens *Diplopora* (Anisian–Ladinian lagoons, cm-tall segmented tufts), *Heteroporella*, *Teutloporella*, *Gyroporella*; solenoporacean reds on reefs; *Tubiphytes* crusts; cyanobacterial mats/stromatolites on flats. A lagoon floor of *Diplopora* "grass" is the best-attested Triassic underwater vegetation.

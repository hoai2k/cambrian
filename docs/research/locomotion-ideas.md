# Locomotion patterns worth stealing

**Built, September 2026, all but the first.** Everything below except *Weight* is in the game now:
the traits live on `CreatureDef` (`src/content/creature-types.ts`), the mechanics in
`src/sim/locomotion.ts` with their hooks in `game.ts`, `flora.ts` and `actors.ts`, and they are
keyed off the creature rather than its era because the same trait turns up in both. `npm run
locomotion` covers the Cambrian bodies and `npm run devonian` the era's own. Each section below
says what it actually landed as.

Research notes, not a plan. The nautiloid work — the stick is always the direction of travel, and a
shell leads with whichever end it is already pointing — asked the obvious next question: which other
animals on the two rosters moved in a way the engine does not model yet, and which of those would be
worth feeling under the hand?

Confidence is marked throughout, because half of this roster is known from flattened fossils and the
locomotion is inference. **Observed** is a living animal you can film. **Well supported** is an
extinct animal whose mechanics are constrained by preserved anatomy and generally agreed. **Inferred**
is a reasonable reading that specialists still argue about; treat those as licence for a game
mechanic, not as fact to put in the codex.

## What the engine already does

So the ideas below can be read against it:

- Camera-relative swimming, sprint, an aimed dash and a neutral-stick dash, dodge, rise and sink.
- The Devonian swim model (`src/sim/devonian/swim.ts`): reverse is slow, the turn sharpens when slow,
  the first sprint from rest is a C-start, and shells are exempt from all of it.
- Shells: free rise and sink, no slow direction, and they swim both ways round without turning.
- Breaching: a fish driving at the surface leaves the water on a ballistic arc and splashes back.
- Ground bodies: paddle when off the floor, climb and glide over rock, hop with rise, shore reach.
- Burrowing and sand ambush; enrolment that rolls downhill and with the current.
- Plants bend and drag on a body pushing through them; rocks collide as the shape they are drawn as.
- A current field that pushes every body (`curK` 0.55 swimming, 0.08 on the floor), and a day cycle.
- Clinging to another creature and lying along its heading (`rideHost`).

Two things stand out as *missing* by their absence: **nothing sinks** — every body hovers for free —
and **every body's heading is its direction of travel**, which is now true of everything except the
shells.

## The candidates, best first

### 1. Weight. Some bodies sink when they stop swimming
> **Not built.** Held back deliberately: it changes every fight and every chase at once, and the
> rest of this list can be judged without it.

*Cladoselache, Stethacanthus, Dunkleosteus, the placoderms — against Michelinoceras, Manticoceras,
Cheirolepis.* **Observed** in living sharks: no swim bladder, a body denser than seawater, and lift
generated by the pectoral fins and heterocercal tail only while moving. Ray-finned fish with a gas
bladder hover for nothing; the chambered shell of a nautiloid is a buoyancy device and does the same.

The mechanic is one line and changes how a whole species feels: below some fraction of cruise speed a
heavy body loses height, and holding station costs stamina. It makes the sea three-dimensional in a
way it is not today — resting means finding the floor or a ledge, ambushing from above is a real
choice because you fall into it for free, and the shells' existing free hover stops being a footnote
and becomes the reason to play one. It also gives the deep water a character: a heavy body that stalls
over the basin has a long way to fall.

Cheapest big win on this list, and it plugs into the buoyancy split the roster already has.

### 2. The tail-flip escape
> **Built** as `tailFlip`: Waptia, Isoxys, Odaraia and Nahecaris. It replaces the dash *and* the
> dodge — whichever button asks, the animal snaps straight back along its own axis whatever the
> stick wanted, harder than an ordinary dash, unsteerable while it flies, and scaled by what is
> left in the tail so a third flip in a row barely clears the ground.

*Nahecaris, Waptia, Isoxys, Odaraia — any bivalved or shrimp-shaped arthropod.* **Observed** as the
caridoid escape reaction in living crustaceans: a giant-fibre reflex that snaps the abdomen under the
body and throws the animal violently backwards, away from whatever touched it, in a few tens of
milliseconds. It is ballistic and barely steerable; repeated flips get weaker as the muscle runs down.

This is the arthropod counterpart of the nautiloid jet and would reuse the plumbing just built: a
committed backwards displacement along the body's own axis, no steering during it, heavy stamina, and
a visible cost to chaining them. It gives the small bodies a real escape that is not just "dash away
faster", and it reads instantly — the animal snaps away from you rather than turning to flee.

### 3. Bodies that never turn to move
> **Built** as `swimStyle: 'omnidirectional'`: Ctenorhabdotus and Furcaster. The heading is cut
> loose from the stick entirely — they translate without rotating, and only aiming points them.
> Furcaster also shapes itself to what it is on (`conformArms`, `src/render/conform.ts`): the arms
> drape over a boulder, dip into the hollow behind it, and curl round anything the animal takes
> hold of. That part is presentation, bent onto the authored clip after the mixer has run.

*Furcaster (brittle star), Ctenorhabdotus (ctenophore), and arguably Burgessomedusa.* **Observed**:
a brittle star picks one arm as the leader and rows with the two beside it, so it changes direction by
choosing a different arm rather than by rotating its disc; a ctenophore's comb rows beat in any
direction and reverse on the spot. Neither animal has a front in the sense the rest of the roster does.

Generalise the shell rule all the way: the heading is decoupled from travel entirely. The body drifts
to face wherever it likes (a slow idle rotation, or facing what it is looking at) while the stick
translates it. Strafing becomes the native gait for these two rather than a penalty, and it makes them
feel *alien* next to the fish, which is exactly what they were.

### 4. Pulse and glide
> **Built** as `swimStyle: 'pulse'`: Burgessomedusa. Thrust arrives as a contraction over the
> first third of a 1.15 s cycle and nothing at all through the refill, so the animal surges and
> coasts; sprint pressed while the bell is refilling fires the contraction *now* instead of waiting
> for the beat, which is the rhythm in it. Its average speed is unchanged — it just arrives in
> lumps. Since 10 September the animation is the same thing rather than a loop running near it:
> the `Swim` clip is exactly one cycle long with its squeeze filling exactly the thrust window, and
> `src/render/creature.ts` scrubs the clip to the actor's own `pulseT` (`bellPhase`), so the bell
> closing on screen is the water being thrown. Asking for nothing pins the phase at zero and the
> animal falls back to `Idle`, the same beat at a third of the size and half the rate. The bell
> also carries itself: `bellTilt` turns the apex into the direction of travel while it beats, and
> it eases back upright when it is sinking, drifting or holding station — climbing needs no tilt,
> because upright already points where it is going. `npm run locomotion` covers all of it.

*Burgessomedusa, and to a lesser extent Ctenorhabdotus.* **Observed** in living medusae: swimming is
discrete bell contractions with a passive relaxation between them, and the animal recaptures the
starting vortex during the refill, which is why jellyfish are among the most efficient swimmers
measured. Thrust is not continuous; there is nothing to hold down.

Movement on a rhythm rather than a level: each pulse is an impulse and the glide between is free, so a
player who taps in time with the bell travels further than one holding the stick. It is the only idea
here that changes the *input*, which makes it the riskiest and the most interesting. It also fits an
animal that should be slow but nearly untiring.

### 5. Two gaits: row and walk
> **Built** as `rowWalk` (Jaekelopterus) and `punt` (Acanthostega, Bothriolepis). A rower keeps
> its sprint and dash off the floor, where everything else with legs is reduced to a doggy paddle,
> and it takes a swimmer's share of the current up there against a walker's on the bottom. A punt
> is worth half again as much as an ordinary dash with the floor in reach, and less than half of
> one without it.

*Jaekelopterus (row with the paddles, walk on the legs), Bothriolepis and Acanthostega (punt off the
bottom on stiff appendages).* **Well supported** for eurypterids, whose last pair of appendages are
flattened paddles on an otherwise walking body; **inferred** for Bothriolepis, whose jointed pectoral
appendages are usually read as props for shuffling or propping on the bottom, and for Acanthostega,
which is now generally reconstructed as aquatic with limbs used to push through weed rather than to
walk on land.

A toggle, not a modifier: rowing is fast and loose in open water, walking is slow but holds station
against the current and is quiet. The current field already exists and nothing yet trades against it.
Punting — push off the floor for a free glide, only available with the bottom in reach — is the same
idea compressed into one button and would suit the armoured bottom-dwellers well.

### 6. Weed-walking
> **Built** as `weedWalk`: the trilobites, Marrella, Sidneyia, Hallucigenia, Furcaster,
> Jaekelopterus, Tiktaalik and Acanthostega. These bodies meet a plant with the leverage of
> something three times their size, so soft growth bends aside instead of pushing them out, and
> what would have dragged on them pulls them along instead. The pull is an acceleration rather than
> a percentage — it settles against the damping every body already has, so it cannot compound step
> on step, and it is worth the same whether the animal is strolling or sprinting. A trilobite now
> crosses a weed bed faster than the same stretch of open water while a swimmer is halved by it.
> Leverage is not immunity: a sponge the size of the animal is still something to climb.

*Acanthostega, Tiktaalik, the trilobites, anything with legs.* **Inferred**, and the most attractive
inversion on the list: the plant drag the engine already applies to a body pushing through flora is a
swimmer's problem. An animal that hauls itself through the stems hand over hand should be *faster* in
thick cover than in open water, not slower. Flip the sign of `resolveFlora`'s drag for limbed bodies
and the crinoid meadows and reed beds become their territory — a place to escape into rather than a
place to be slowed in. It costs almost nothing and it gives the shallows a resident.

### 7. Surfaces, not just the floor
> **Built in part** as `cling`: Odontogriphus, Wiwaxia and Hallucigenia. No rock face is a cliff
> to them, however steep or however far above — they go up and over the stacks and the arch piers
> that everything else swims around. What is *not* built is the rest of the idea: they still stand
> the world's way up rather than the surface's, so there is no walking along an overhang upside
> down. That needs a surface normal and a camera that tolerates being sideways, and it is worth
> doing on its own rather than smuggled in here.

*Odontogriphus, Hallucigenia, the grazing molluscs.* **Observed** in living gastropods and
onychophorans: a mucus-adhesive foot or clinging claws will hold a body on vertical rock and on the
underside of an overhang; the animal's "down" is the surface, not the world.

The prop-footprint work means the game now knows the shape of every rock and landmark. A crawler that
sticks to those surfaces would traverse the arch's underside and the stack's flanks while everything
else swims round them, and it would make the landmarks structure rather than scenery. This is the most
expensive idea here — it needs a surface normal, a camera that tolerates being sideways, and an
answer for what happens when you let go — but it is also the one that would change how the world
reads.

### 8. Restricted control surfaces
> **Built** as `pitchRate`: Doryaspis (0.45 rad/s) and Bothriolepis (0.5). Their pitch is rate
> limited rather than damped, so a climb or a dive is a line they commit to.

*Doryaspis, Bothriolepis, the armoured jawless fish.* **Inferred**: a rigid head shield with no paired
fins behind it leaves the tail as the only motor, and the extended rostrum of Doryaspis is often read
as a hydrofoil or a sediment tool. Whatever it did, the animal cannot have pitched and rolled the way a
finned fish does.

Cap the pitch rate rather than the speed: these bodies climb and dive slowly and have to commit to a
line, so getting to the surface for air, or off the floor away from something, becomes a piece of
navigation rather than a stick flick. It is a restriction that makes the water column feel deep.

### 9. Ram feeding needs speed
> **Built** as `ramFeed`: Titanichthys, Tamisiocaris and Odaraia. Below a quarter of cruise the
> net strains nothing at all.

*Titanichthys, Tamisiocaris, Odaraia.* **Well supported** for large filter feeders: the mouth is a net
and the net only works if water is going through it. Feeding while stationary should do nothing;
feeding while cruising should be the point of the animal. The filter abilities exist already
(`filterGulp`, `planktonComb`, `collectorWake`) — this is a condition on them, not a new system, and
it would stop the filterers being played as slow brawlers.

### 10. Going limp in the current
> **Built** as `drift`: Burgessomedusa and Ctenorhabdotus. On a neutral stick the sea moves them
> about three times harder than it moves a swimmer, and they climb through the night and sink
> through the day.

*The drifters: Burgessomedusa, Ctenorhabdotus, larvae of everything.* **Observed** — most plankton
does not swim horizontally at all; it holds a depth and lets the water do the rest, and the classic
behaviour is a vertical migration, rising at dusk and sinking at dawn.

The current field and the day cycle are both already there and neither is a *choice* yet. A mode that
gives up steering in exchange for free speed along the current, and an ambient pull up at night and
down by day for the soft-bodied animals, would make the water feel like it is going somewhere. It also
pairs with the sinking in idea 1: the column stops being a flat plane you swim about on.

## What I would not bother with

- **Metachronal flap waves** (Anomalocaris, Cambroraster, the radiodonts). Real and well supported —
  a travelling wave down the lateral flaps — but at the scale the camera sits it would read as an
  animation detail, not a control difference, and the animation already carries it.
- **Undulation modes** (anguilliform vs carangiform vs ostraciiform). The difference between Pikaia's
  whole-body ripple and Cheirolepis' tail-only beat is a real one, but it lands as speed and turn-rate
  numbers, which the stats generator already produces from body length and body-lengths-per-second.
- **Autotomy** (Furcaster shedding an arm to escape a grip). Lovely, real, and it is a combat mechanic
  rather than a movement one — it belongs with the grab and guard work, not here.

## What is left

Sinking (1), which was held back on purpose, and the half of surface-crawling (7) that needs the
body to stand the surface's way up rather than the world's.

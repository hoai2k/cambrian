# Tiktaalik roseae: anatomical decisions

This is an original reconstruction, not a fossil scan. Representative scale will be 2 m; proportions, soft tissue, pigmentation, tail outline and motion remain reconstructions. No existing Tiktaalik V1 was present to preserve.

Primary references inspected locally:
- Daeschler, Shubin & Jenkins (2006), *A Devonian tetrapod-like fish and the evolution of the tetrapod body plan*. DOI https://doi.org/10.1038/nature04639 — figures 2–6, skull, dentary and rhombic narrowly overlapping tuberculated scales. Tiktaalik's snout is 62% of tip-to-postparietal length; dorsal eyes sit closely together toward the rear of its triangular flattened skull. No anterior dorsal fin interrupts the scale coat. Small marginal teeth and larger dentary/coronoid teeth are shown; no modern crocodile dental pattern is implied.
- Shubin, Daeschler & Jenkins (2006), *The pectoral fin of Tiktaalik roseae and the origin of the tetrapod limb*. DOI https://doi.org/10.1038/nature04637 — figures 2 and 7 show a stout humerus, radius/ulna, distal mesomeres/radials, and a substantial lepidotrichial web. Shoulder, elbow and distal zones can form an aquatic prop pose. No fingers or exposed finger-like rays.
- Lemberg, Daeschler & Shubin (2021), *The feeding system of Tiktaalik roseae: an intermediate between suction feeding and biting*. DOI https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2016421118; https://knowledge.uchicago.edu/records/s34bv-j5m06 — figures 1–3 show a rigid central skull/braincase, separate cheek-palate modules and lateral sliding scarf joints. Modest cheek expansion is appropriate; bending the entire skull is not. Palate regions remain separated by the midline braincase. The animation is illustrative, not measured fossil kinematics.
- Stewart et al. (2024), *The axial skeleton of Tiktaalik roseae*. DOI https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2316106121; https://knowledge.uchicago.edu/records/mvb59-qth34 — figures 7–8 support a short mobile neck, expanded pelvis and pelvic fins approaching pectoral size. Ligamentous sacral support is inferred, not a fused tetrapod hip. Posterior-facing hip articulation constrains retraction; ordinary terrestrial walking is not asserted. Aquatic paddling and submerged propping are appropriate. The reconstructed complete tail silhouette is less certain than the preserved trunk.
- Stewart et al. (2020), *Fin ray patterns at the fin-to-limb transition*. DOI https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1915983117 — substantial ventral muscular coverage and asymmetric fin-ray covering support a fleshy distal support surface inside a fin web.

Geometry direction: flattened triangular head, low rounded snout and shallow long jaws, dorsal eyes seated in real cranial volume, a short flexible neck and rib-supported trunk, four robust fins with jointed muscular proximal zones and thinner continuous ray webs. Conservative low tapering tail and caudal web. No shark dorsal fins, modern limb digits, decorative orbital hoops, exposed fin-ray cords, or external gill bars. Head roof relief is continuous skin, not stacked plates. Oral roof, floor and cheek lining are real contiguous three-dimensional surfaces.

Motion direction: body/tail undulation, paddle recovery, asymmetric fin braking, modest neck aiming, suction-compatible gape/cheek expansion, and buoyancy-supported substrate bracing. The runtime's action names describe compatible gestures, not a declaration of Devonian gameplay. Growth replaces Moult.

References remain local for study. Copyrighted fossil figures are not model textures and will not be shipped.
